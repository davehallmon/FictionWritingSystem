# Source-Text Character Fundamentals Analysis Criteria

> Load when assessing protagonist, supporting-character, or antagonist design. Review the decision route first, select the correct template, and then follow its steps. This is a primary output reference for character-designer.

## Use as Deconstruction Criteria

This file began as a writing-side playbook phrased as “how to write.” During deconstruction, `story-short-analyze` uses it as a
**comparative benchmark**: compare the source text with the standard patterns described here to identify which one it uses and how effectively it uses it. **Do not** use this file to write a new work during analysis.

From a reader’s perspective, the patterns here are industrial conventions for popular short fiction such as Fanqie short stories, Story Club tales, wife-chasing redemption, social-condition stories, rebirth, and revenge against abusers. They serve as the comparative source for “reusable structure / reversal design / genre formulas” and similar sections of the deconstruction report.

## Section 1: Protagonist Card

### Fill-in Template

```
姓名：
性别：
角色定位：（一句话说清主角在故事中的功能）
身份标签：（如：废柴大学生、前特种兵、落魄皇子）
外貌特征：（3-5 个关键词，要有记忆点）
  例：瘦高、总穿旧夹克、左手有疤、眼神懒散
性格关键词：（3-5 个，必须有矛盾面）
  例：嘴毒心软、看似冷漠实则护短、冲动但有底线
核心目标：（全书终点想达成什么）
核心动机：（必须达成的理由——情感驱动，非理性驱动）
致命弱点：（让角色犯错的性格缺陷，非能力缺陷）
口头禅/标志动作：（让读者秒认的标签）
```

### Design Essentials

- Motivation must operate at the emotional level—“avenge their mother” is stronger than “become the strongest.”
- A weakness must cause the protagonist to make a mistake at a crucial moment; otherwise it is not a weakness.
- Appearance and catchphrases are reader-memory anchors and must be specified.

### Design Essentials

- Every supporting character needs a specific function. A character without one should not appear.
- Plan supporting-character exits deliberately; do not forget characters as the story progresses.
- No more than three supporting characters should have dialogue in one scene.

## Section 3: Antagonist Design

### 3.1 Antagonist Tier Table

Select the tier based on the antagonist’s page time and design to the table.

#### Minor Antagonist (1–5 Chapters)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Obstacle within one small arc |
| Design | One or two vivid traits are enough (arrogant/greedy/bullies the weak and fears the strong) |
| Exit | Defeated or exposed cleanly and decisively |

#### Mid-Level Antagonist (10–30 Chapters)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Primary opponent for one volume |
| Motivation | Why they oppose the protagonist (cannot be “pure evil”) |
| Methods | Force/scheming/resources |
| Presence | Defeats the protagonist at least once, making readers hate them viscerally |
| Exit | Directly defeated by the protagonist, with a satisfying payoff |

#### Major-Arc Boss (One or More Volumes)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Embodies the core conflict of one stage |
| Character arc | Has a complete arc, goals, and logic |
| Conflict | Ideological conflict with the protagonist, not merely conflicting interests |
| Confrontation | Drives the protagonist into a desperate position at least once |
| Other side | Has one aspect readers cannot bring themselves to hate |
| Exit | A climactic final battle with ceremonial weight, followed by a resonant ending |

#### Final Boss (Entire Work)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Personifies the work’s central conflict |
| Foreshadowing | Present from Chapter 1 |
| Opposition | Directly opposes the protagonist’s goal/motivation and represents the thematic inverse |
| Power | Overwhelms the protagonist; victory requires transformation |
| Conviction | Holds a belief; is not merely a mad person or monster |

### 3.2 Iron Rules of Antagonist Design

- The antagonist’s intelligence and strength determine the protagonist’s worth. A weak antagonist makes the protagonist’s victory meaningless.
- The antagonist’s behavior must follow internal logic that makes sense from their perspective.
- Never reduce the antagonist’s intelligence merely to hand the protagonist a victory.

### 3.3 Four Elements for Establishing an Antagonist

Apply these in order; none may be omitted:

1. **Demonstrate capability**—show strength or methods immediately upon entry.
2. **Credible motivation**—make the behavior coherent from the antagonist’s perspective.
3. **Real threat**—have the antagonist defeat the protagonist at least once.
4. **Timing the ultimate intent**—reserve the true objective for a key reversal.

### 3.4 Four-Step Method for Establishing Antagonist Personality

| Step | Action |
|------|------|
| 1. The antagonist also has a dream | In their own eyes, they are the protagonist of their story |
| 2. Unearth a traumatic past | Who raised them? Who loved them? Who hurt them? What pain do they avoid? |
| 3. Hone the character flaw | Under adversity, they do not activate a positive trait but hone the flaw—the “strength” itself becomes fatal |
| 4. The antagonist mirrors the protagonist | The antagonist’s strengths expose the protagonist’s weaknesses; their complete opposition guarantees friction whenever they meet |

### 3.5 The Key to Giving an Antagonist Weight

- Obstacles arise from the antagonist’s characterization, not from intentionally placing obstacles before the protagonist.
- Antagonist-weight formula: clear motivation → motivation drives behavior → behavior creates an obstacle → obstacle intensity comes from the antagonist’s weight.
- When the antagonist is exceptionally dominant, the protagonist’s solution should feel “advanced,” “intelligent,” and “unexpected.”

## Section 4: Motivation Design

### 4.1 Motivation-Chain Template

```
起因（Cause）    → 角色经历了什么
意图（Intent）   → 角色想做什么
约束（Constraint）→ 什么在阻碍他
风险（Risk）     → 失败的代价是什么
```

**Example**:
```
起因：母亲被仇家杀害
意图：找到凶手并复仇
约束：凶手是当朝权臣，主角只是平民
风险：复仇失败会被灭门，复仇成功会失去所有现有生活
```

### 4.2 Motivation-Chain Design Essentials

- The cause must be specific. “Was bullied” is insufficient; “was slapped in public” is specific enough.
- The constraint must be powerful; otherwise the protagonist steamrolls every obstacle and the story lacks tension.
- The risk must be real: readers must believe the protagonist may truly lose something important.
- Motivation may evolve with the plot, but it cannot change without cause.

### 4.3 Types of Motivational Conflict

When a character needs an internal struggle, select one type from this table:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Goal vs. morality | Conflict between what the character wants and ought to do | Seek revenge without harming the innocent |
| Emotion vs. interest | Tension between emotion and reason | Knows the other person is unreliable but cannot let go |
| Short term vs. long term | Conflict between immediate benefit and a distant objective | Compromise to survive vs. uphold a principle and wager on the future |
| Self vs. others | Conflict between personal needs and others’ expectations | Wants a quiet life but bears a family mission |

## Section 5: Character-Development Methods Quick Reference

### 5.1 Core Principles of Character Development

**Three requirements for protagonist behavior**:
1. **Understandable**—readers must understand why the protagonist acts.
2. **Relatable**—readers must be able to identify with the protagonist’s thinking.
3. **Acceptable**—readers must be able to accept the protagonist’s values.

**Show rather than tell**:
- Character purpose → show through behavior, not narrator explanation.
- Character action → use specific actions and decisions, not piles of adjectives.
- Character attitude → reveal it through dialogue and reactions, not internal monologue.

### 5.2 Methods for Showing Character Traits

| Method | Action | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Sensitivity | Touch a weak point and observe the response | Panic/rush away/redirect attention indicate different personalities |
| Avoidance | Show avoidance behavior to imply a weakness | What a character “does not do” reveals deeper character more than what they do |
| Strength before flaw | Show an appealing trait before revealing the flaw | Readers must like the character before they will tolerate the flaw |

### 5.3 Four Categories of Personality Attributes

| Attribute category | Definition | Design essential |
|---------|------|---------|
| Moral | Beliefs about right and wrong | Shapes the formation of other traits |
| Achievement | Drives goal attainment | Aligns with morality and serves the objective |
| Interaction | How the character communicates | The largest group of positive traits |
| Identity | Sense of identity | Defines the fundamental components of individuality |

Selection technique: choose one as the primary trait and make the others secondary.

### 5.4 Four-Layer Character-Development Method

| Layer | Method |
|------|------|
| Empathy | Do not introduce too many characters at the opening; first let readers understand the protagonist and form expectations |
| Minimal feature (tag) | Develop the protagonist from the inside out and supporting characters from the outside in, using tags |
| Character insincerity | What is said ≠ what is thought; conduct ≠ attitude; routine and the lunchbox are opposite poles that deepen character |
| Character spotlight | Supporting characters must strongly connect to the protagonist; when one overshadows the protagonist, introduce a new supporting character to take away that halo |

### 5.5 Characterization and Character Truth

| Layer | Definition | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Characterization (manifest) | Traits readers see at first glance | Appears to be a youthful, adorable girl |
| Character truth (latent) | The character’s actual inner nature | Is really a cold-blooded killer |
| Value orientation (implicit) | The values decisive in a critical choice | Chooses ambition over righteousness |

Operational note: values need not be revealed at the beginning. Expose them naturally during a crucial decision. The later the character truth is revealed, the greater the dramatic tension.

### 5.6 Layered Character-Presentation Method

| Layer | Action |
|------|------|
| First | Tell readers the archetype (cute girl/mature woman/young married woman) |
| Second | Apply distinguishing tags (large chest/long legs/wheat-colored skin) |
| Third | Find a cause for the personality (family/internal impulse) |
| Fourth | Break the model—the adorably naïve character actually understands everything, immediately elevating the design |

Deepen memory: do not hesitate to repeat vocabulary. Does it seem overly simple? It is actually reinforcing memory.

### 5.7 Three-Timeline Method for Character Experience

| Timeline | Content |
|--------|------|
| Past | Background, pivotal experiences, and causes of personality |
| Present | Relationship to the protagonist and current interaction pattern |
| Future | Creates motivation to grow or a crisis (mentor abducted/heroine captured/antagonist exposes the truth) |

### 5.8 Supporting-Character Obsession Method

To make readers quickly accept a secondary supporting character:

- Give the character an obsession → organize all behavior, interests, and personality around it.
- Not every appearance must express the obsession. Avoid contradicting it and reveal it at key moments.
- If the obsession is too extreme → add a small relatable detail → make reader acceptance easier.
- Best for secondary supporting characters, not frequently appearing major ones.

### 5.9 Repeated Behavioral Motifs

A key technique for preserving character integrity in long-form work:

- Select one behavioral trait and write it repeatedly.
- Construction method: identify the type readers enjoy → make it concrete as behavior → repeat it in different scenes.
- Character appeal + core story appeal = depth; cycle the two to produce differentiation.
- Antagonists and supporting characters also need recurring appeal. An antagonist readers can love increases freshness.

### 5.10 Write Behavior Around the Character Design

Behavior, language, and thought must all arise from personality. If the plot requires behavior that violates the established characterization → revise the plot first, not the character design.

### 5.11 Character Drives Event vs. Event Drives Character

| Driver | Characteristics |
|---------|------|
| Character drives event | Plot follows naturally from personality, motivation, and choice, deepening the character arc |
| Event drives character | An external event breaks equilibrium and forces the character to reveal their true self |

When stuck → seek direction in character motivation; do not force a plot event.

## Section 6: Special Protagonist Problems

### 6.1 Protagonist Presence

- The protagonist must have presence; it is the foundation for reader identification.
- Avoid excessive unintelligent internal monologue and profanity.
- The protagonist may be imperfect but must not invite contempt.

### 6.2 Protagonist Growth

- Growth appears not only in power but also in judgment and choices.
- Every instance of growth needs a triggering event and internal reflection.
- Growth is not mandatory for a web-fiction protagonist: an ordinary person with mature values at the opening is easier to identify with, and consistent characterization is itself appealing.

### 6.3 Protagonist Red Lines

Never write the following protagonist types:

| Prohibited | Reason |
|------|------|
| Sanctimonious self-sacrificer | Readers want catharsis, not moral lecturing |
| Mindless fighting machine | Difficult to connect with emotionally |
| Evil at the core | Readers cannot identify with them |
| Makes mistakes through stupidity or misplaced mercy | Readers cannot forgive them |
| Gives up on themself | More repellent to readers than being bullied |

### 6.4 What to Do When the Protagonist Starts Too High

**Warning signs**:
- No room for growth—ability/status/resources have reached their ceiling.
- The plot lacks tension and falls into a repetitive “cutting through everything” cycle.
- Readers lose the motivation to continue.

**Solutions**:
- **Suppress momentum, not the person**—lower the aura and expectations without diminishing the protagonist’s ability or dignity.
- **Leave dimensional gaps**—however strong the protagonist is in one area, weaknesses remain in others.
- **Overachievement method**—what first appears to be a major gain proves even greater after the protagonist calculates it.

### 6.5 Establishing the Protagonist at the Opening

- Characterization is established through choice; the protagonist must make a choice in the opening.
- An opening built around a battle of wits establishes character more readily than one built around violence.
- Readers identify more easily with a “clever but ordinary” protagonist than with one who begins invincible.

### 6.6 The Protagonist’s Two Identities and Two Advantages

| Dimension | Definition | Function |
|------|------|------|
| Manifest identity | Social identity/occupation plus state | Concentrates early conflicts and must continually evolve or be replaced |
| Hidden identity | Exceptional origin | Concentrates middle- and late-stage conflicts |
| Manifest advantage | System/jade pendant/rebirth memories | Acquired at the opening and persists throughout the work |
| Hidden advantage | The protagonist’s personality | What makes the protagonist distinctive is itself an advantage |

**Best practice**: use the advantage to demonstrate superiority early → progressively clarify the characterization as the plot advances.
**Characterization and the work’s overall tone**: the protagonist must fit the world’s tone, tightly unifying four elements: social identity + origin + advantage + personality.

### 6.7 Single-Flaw Protagonist plus Animal Companions

If the protagonist is intentionally given a narrow personality → reduce reader resistance to identification → fewer actions mean fewer errors → maximize identification.

**Problem**: overly narrow personality → less excitement → difficulty advancing the story.
**Solution**: give the emotionally reserved protagonist three to five animal companions with different personalities.

| Companion type | Function |
|---------|------|
| Silly and adorably foolish | Provides comedy; the protagonist should not suffer humiliations, but the animal can |
| Clever and sharp-tongued | Serves as the protagonist’s mouthpiece, saying what the protagonist should not and preserving their presence |
| Straightforward and slow-witted | Provides contrast-driven charm |
| Timid with unusual powers | Supplements abilities |

### 6.8 Balancing Core Emotion and the Urge to Express

- The underlying need stays constant; only its form changes.
- After writing a plot passage, review it from the reader’s perspective for drift from the core trope.
- When the protagonist proves exceptional, they must gain something; they cannot only pay costs without reward.
- Drift is most likely when the urge to express is strong—check proactively.

### 6.9 Suppress Momentum, Not the Person: The Correct Source of Conflict

**Wrong approach**: make the antagonist target the protagonist for no reason, without defensible underlying logic.

**Correct approach**: the antagonist pursues an independent goal whose actions objectively create the protagonist’s predicament, rather than deliberately targeting the protagonist personally.

**Conflict chain**: antagonist’s goal → suppresses the group containing the protagonist (suppresses momentum) → protagonist overthrows it → people at the bottom remain numb → protagonist changes approach → new conflict arises naturally.

Core principle: everyone is driven by the benefit they seek. Behind that are different values, deeper still are formative environments, and deepest is worldview.

## Section 7: Deeper Antagonist Development

### 7.1 Core Principles

- A classic antagonist has a complete worldview and internally coherent values.
- The same external event changes different people differently; external and internal causes resonate.
- Antagonist development cannot consist only of external events; it needs internal values and logic.

### 7.2 Writing the Antagonist as a Protagonist

**Traditional problem**: after a setting change, the original antagonist cannot keep pace → a new, stereotyped antagonist is introduced.

**Solution**:
- Write the antagonist as if they were a protagonist.
- Add “supporting characters enhance supporting characters” to “supporting characters enhance the protagonist.”
- Handle a setting change by using the new setting to demonstrate the established character’s value, not by making the established character powerless merely to demonstrate the new setting’s difficulty.

## Quality Checklist

After completing the character design, check each item:

### Protagonist

- [ ] Motivation is emotional, not an empty phrase such as “become the strongest.”
- [ ] Has a fatal weakness that causes a mistake at a crucial moment.
- [ ] Appearance has memorable features (three to five keywords).
- [ ] Has a catchphrase or signature action.
- [ ] Maintains presence and does not invite reader contempt.
- [ ] Does not cross a red line (sanctimonious self-sacrificer/mindless fighting machine/evil core/stupid mistake/giving up).
- [ ] Core objective aligns with the main story line.

### Supporting Character

- [ ] Has a specific function (mentor/ally/information source/sacrifice/mirror).
- [ ] One or two keywords summarize the core traits.
- [ ] Has a signature feature readers remember.
- [ ] Exit has been planned.
- [ ] No more than three supporting characters speak in one scene.

### Antagonist

- [ ] Designed according to the tier table, with scope matching tier.
- [ ] Demonstrates strength or methods upon entry.
- [ ] Motivation makes sense from the antagonist’s perspective.
- [ ] Defeats the protagonist at least once (mid-level and above).
- [ ] Does not lose through reduced intelligence.
- [ ] A major-arc Boss has an ideological conflict, not merely conflicting interests.
- [ ] The final Boss is foreshadowed from Chapter 1.

### Motivation

- [ ] Cause is specific, not vague language such as “was bullied.”
- [ ] Constraint is powerful; the protagonist cannot simply steamroll everything.
- [ ] Risk is real; readers believe the protagonist may lose something important.
- [ ] Motivational evolution is set up and does not happen without cause.

### Development

- [ ] Character purpose is shown through behavior, not narrator explanation.
- [ ] Behavior/language/thought follows the character design; the characterization is not changed for plot convenience.
- [ ] Major characters have repeated behavioral motifs that maintain long-form consistency.
- [ ] Supporting characters clearly connect to the protagonist without stealing the spotlight.
- [ ] Character truth/value orientation emerges naturally during a crucial decision.
