## When Used as an Analysis Rubric

This file was originally a writing-side playbook framed as “how to write.” During analysis, `story-short-analyze` uses it as a **comparison rubric**: compare the source text with the standard patterns described here to identify which techniques it uses and how effectively it uses them. Do **not** use this file to write a new work during analysis.

From a reader’s perspective, this file describes industrial-standard templates used in popular short fiction, including Tomato Novel stories, Story Club fiction, wife-chasing redemption, social drama, rebirth fiction, and revenge stories. It provides the comparison basis for report sections such as “reusable structure,” “reversal design,” and “genre formula.”

## General Writing Rules

### Central-Hook Method

Central hook = a complete emotional chain: specific expectation → specific satisfaction.

**Three-layer central-hook design**:

- First generation = theme/central idea, such as changing destiny
- Second generation = genre core, such as progression through cultivation
- Third generation = core emotion, such as proving that an underestimated youth must not be despised

**Construction order**: Central selling point → emotional pattern → plot designed around the pattern

### Three-Layer Contrast-Label Characterization

| Layer | Explanation | Example |
|----|------|------|
| Identity label | Popular stereotype | Holy Maiden of the Joyous Union Sect → femme fatale |
| Presentation label | Trait shown in ordinary relationships | Aloof and untouched by worldly concerns |
| Inner label | Revealed by crisis or intimacy | Fundamentally kind and frugal |

Greater contrast is better. After a character enters the third layer, they can no longer behave exactly as the first and second layers imply.

### Showing Is Better Than Telling

Showing must include setup, expectation, and payoff; a character’s purpose, action, and attitude; and the plot’s setup, reversal, and aftermath.

### Event-Driven vs. Emotion-Driven

| Type | Driving Source | Reader’s Question | Characteristics |
|------|----------|----------|------|
| Event-driven | External conflict | Can they win? | Fast pace and clear payoffs |
| Emotion-driven | Internal conflict | Why do they think this way? | Sustained push-pull tension and character suspense |

Event suspense is temporary; character suspense persists.

### Conflict-Introduction Formula

[Antagonist characterization] + [motive] + [behavior] + [attitude] + [degree of harm to the protagonist]

Three conflict types:

1. **Resource seizure** — The antagonist needs a resource and transfers the cost to the protagonist.
2. **Consequence transfer** — The antagonist is punished, but the protagonist bears the cost.
3. **Direct opposition** — The most intense form: a protagonist trait disrupts the antagonist’s established order.

### Narrative-Unit System

| Emotional Module | Core | Required Elements | Source of Payoff |
|--------|------|----------|----------|
| Heroic rescue | Relationship transformation | Crisis → rescue → improved opinion | Rescued character’s increased affection |
| Status display | Contrast | Ability demonstrated → others shocked | Sense of superiority |
| Risking little for much | Profitable gamble | Small cost → struggle → large return | Satisfaction of gaining an advantage |
| Youthful heroism | Acting despite impossible odds | Injustice → good person suffers → resolve → success | Sense of justice |
| Called upon in crisis | Surpassing everyone | Sudden crisis → others fail → protagonist intervenes | Lone-hero satisfaction |
| Good deeds rewarded | Good receives good | Good deed → hinted reward → payoff | Moral causality |

**Core principle**: Dramatic novelty wears out; emotion does not.

Combination rule: Upward modules may stand alone. A downward module must be followed by an upward one. Combined modules are stronger than isolated ones.

### Three-Step Progression Design

1. **List the starting point** — The protagonist’s current identity, resources, and circumstances.
2. **List the ending point** — What the protagonist gains by the scene’s end.
3. **Work backward to create the emotional gap** — Establish the need for that final gain earlier.

| Final Gain | Required Setup |
|----------|----------|
| Critical evidence | A supporting character emphasizes its scarcity |
| Higher status | Demonstrate the power and authority of that status |
| Help from a character | Demonstrate that character’s value |
| Awakening of a special advantage | Demonstrate the protagonist’s previous helplessness |

**Multi-angle comparison**: Show the difference before and after progression through four dimensions—damage output, social status, others’ opinions, and the protagonist’s own experience.

### Opening Techniques

| Technique | Explanation | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Pressured while ill | Force the most exhausting demand on someone at their weakest | “I had just undergone spinal surgery... but Mom gripped my wrist. ‘Hurry. The ceremony is about to begin.’” |
| Anomalous object | An object that should not exist triggers the entire story | “The date on the condom suddenly changed, and so did the flavor—to mango, which I’m allergic to.” |
| Watching one’s death | The spirit rises in the first sentence | “My spirit slowly floated upward as I watched Mom’s face turn ashen.” |
| Public betrayal | The protagonist is betrayed before everyone in the opening sentence | “The little sister I raised passed over me with a pen authorizing a ten-billion-yuan deal.” |
| Feigned compliance | After rebirth, willingly surrender what the protagonist died protecting previously | “If you want it, take it all. Whoever gets it will face disaster anyway.” |
| Death retrospective | State the manner of death and culprit immediately | “Eight months pregnant, I discovered my husband’s affair. The shock caused premature labor, and I hemorrhaged on the operating table.” |
| Cold discovery | Respond to an anomaly with a cold smile instead of tears | “I smiled and nodded. That day, I booked a postpartum care center, then sent a report to Legal.” |

### Advanced Emotion-Control Techniques

1. **Escalating betrayal**: Record how betrayal escalates by category, cost, or relational consequence—moral → financial → mortal—without requiring one disclosure per chapter.
2. **Warm-cold contrast**: Analyze how leverage, choices, and consequences create contrast. Do not assume the protagonist must remain cold while the other person becomes hysterical.
3. **Peeling the two-faced character**: Present a kind surface, then strip away the truth layer by layer, such as care expressed through copied-and-pasted messages.
4. **Interwoven previous-life memories**: Record how a previous-life memory changes current judgment, choices, or information. Do not treat one memory per chapter as a quality requirement.
5. **Trial-style dialogue**: Determine whether questions force a choice, expose a contradiction, or change a relationship. Repeated interrogation without leverage is not an effective technique.

### Structural-Innovation Techniques

1. **Short-chapter progression**: Observe whether section length serves a complete action chain and whether revelations, choices, information, or consequences keep advancing. Do not treat fixed chapter lengths or numbers of explosive moments as quality requirements.
2. **Recurring clue-object**: Carry one object through the story with both emotional and plot functions.
3. **Spatial-temporal contrast**: Contrast two places at the same moment—for example, a brother is beaten while a cheating man helps his mistress choose a private jet.
4. **Time-bomb evidence**: Evidence set by the protagonist before death is sent automatically and detonates at the critical moment.

### Advanced Ending Techniques

1. **Warm rebirth**: The protagonist is reborn into a loving family, where warm details answer what was missing before.
2. **Resonant object**: Use an object to imply the future without explaining it.
3. **Long tracking shot**: The protagonist walks toward the doors step by step while everyone behind them reacts in shock.
4. **Ironic counterpoint**: End from the antagonist’s perspective; indifference is more powerful than revenge.

### Dialogue Functions and Boundaries

| Scene Tendency | Dialogue’s Main Function | Engine When There Is No Dialogue |
|----------|--------------|--------------------|
| Emotional push-pull / relational contest | Change information, strategy, power, relationship, or the next action | Choices, objects, evidence, spatial distance, or consequences |

Treat dialogue share only as descriptive data for the text and comparable samples from the same genre and platform. Do not impose a passing threshold for the entire story. Also explain what advances scenes without dialogue.

### Signature-Line Templates

| Scenario | Signature Line |
|------|------|
| Calm judgment | “Your humanity is not meant to excuse betrayal and deception. My warmth is precious; I reserve it for people who deserve it.” |
| Final break | “Blood, it turns out, cannot be trusted.” |
| Identity revealed | “Nonsense! The head of my Shen family is clearly Jian Xi.” |
| Watching one’s death | “She didn’t know that all I wanted was for her to ask whether it hurt.” |
| Final indifference | “That is their story now. It has nothing more to do with me.” |
| Ironic judgment | “You’re thirty-three, not thirteen. Can ‘I was manipulated’ really erase your responsibility?” |

### Short-Form Writing Boundaries

| Standard | Requirement | Violation |
|------|------|----------|
| Conflict within the first 100 Chinese characters | The first sentence is an information bomb | “On a bright and sunny morning...” |
| Dialogue serves scene function | Changes information, strategy, power, relationship, or action | Adding questions, repetition, or empty threats solely to increase dialogue share |
| Paragraphs serve dramatic units | Finish an event, shot, or chain of reasoning before breaking | Splitting by sentence count until the prose resembles an outline or telegram |
| Emotion words followed by concrete action | When naming an emotion directly, follow it with a specific action or object in the scene, as is common on Tomato Novel, Qimao, and Heiyan; only an emotional summary with nothing concrete after it counts as AI-like | In *My Boyfriend Shared My Private Photos to Please His First Love*: “I pressed my lips together until I tasted blood.” |
| Scenes create real change | Change at least one of risk, information, relationship, resources, decision, action, or reader understanding | Adding bystander reactions, memories, or independent obstacles merely to give a scene multiple functions |
| End with action/dialogue | Do not elevate, summarize, or moralize | “From then on, she understood...” |
| Distinctive protagonist | Record whether composure appears through evidence gathering, choices, delayed responses, or resource allocation, and whether a signature action is unique to this story | Treating generic gestures such as lifting a cup or straightening a suit as mandatory |
| Information and consequences keep advancing | New information, action, relationship changes, local payoffs, or consequences may all carry the progression | Inventing a separate information bomb to make every chapter pass |

**Three strong ending patterns**: A. End from the antagonist’s perspective. B. Use an open-ended implication. C. Close with a ritual-like action.

**Core of rebirth fiction**: The protagonist’s first act is something they would never have done in the previous life.

**Spirit/ghost viewpoint**: Omniscient but unable to intervene. The cruelty of watching is more shocking than direct participation.

## Core Rules of Emotion Control

1. **Proportional emotional release**: The worse the antagonist’s behavior, the more satisfying the counterattack. The positive release should match the negative emotion invested.
2. **Create an emotional gap**: Make readers desire something before satisfying that desire.
3. **Withholding a deserved payoff is worse than a toxic plot point**: Once a trump card appears, the antagonist must suffer its corresponding suppression, and the special advantage’s effect must be unmistakable.
4. **Emotion is the foundation**: Every technique ultimately serves emotion.

### Rules for Information Gaps

- Information gaps are central to anticipation: Readers know something a character does not.
- If the antagonist is vulnerable to the protagonist’s advantage, anticipation compounds.
- The payoff erupts when the information gap closes.
- Emphasize emotional reversals among characters in the relationship network when transmitting the information gap.
- Shocking someone familiar is more satisfying than shocking a stranger.

### Core Toxicity Diagnosis

- Cathartic fiction that is not satisfying becomes toxic; clearly demonstrate the special advantage’s effects.
- Repeatedly violating reader expectations creates a crisis of trust.
- The protagonist’s behavior must be understandable, relatable, and acceptable.
- An antagonist who dies too easily or independently of the protagonist destroys completion rates.
- Except in pure comedy, avoid abrupt jokes that destroy the atmosphere.

## Romance-Arc Rules

### Principles for Advancing Romance Stages

- Progression means advancing the relationship stage: chance acquaintance → first attraction → ambiguity → commitment → deepening.
- Design qualitative changes in affection at the detailed-outline or chapter-outline level.
- In slice-of-life fiction, affection grows gradually through linked small events rather than one dramatic leap.
- Do not write excessive ambiguity early; behavior must match the current stage.

### Core of Slice-of-Life/Romantic-Fluff Fiction

- The core is **detailed interaction** between the leads.
- Build relationship development from characterization.
- Emotional change + detailed interaction = what readers want.
- New setting + stable characterization + recurring intimate contact = an effective cycle.

### Binding Affection to the Career Arc

- Helping the heroine and advancing the career objective must be the same action.
- Career progress should naturally increase affection.
- Career-centered fiction can tolerate a longer period of contact.
- Romance-centered fiction should reach the stage where the heroine actively provides emotional affirmation quickly.

## Female-Reader Psychology and Writing Techniques

### Reader Psychology

1. **Primarily an observer of compelling drama**: Identification is secondary to the shared pleasure of watching interpersonal drama.
2. **Very high need for safety**: The copy or synopsis must imply success or hope.
3. **Sensitive to agency**: A heroine without agency creates greater acquisition and retention risk.
4. **Emotion-product orientation**: Whatever the style, the work is fundamentally an emotional product.

### Concealed-Strength Romance

- Core mechanism: An ability contrast overturns the apparent power relationship and creates sexual tension.
- Concealment requires a clear, credible motive.
- Reveal the ability precisely when the other person most needs protection.
- Greater contrast creates greater tension.
- Plant clues to the hidden identity in advance; it cannot appear suddenly.

### Absurdist Comedy Style in Female-Audience Cultivation/Historical Romance

- Character interaction, reactions, and adapted memes are the main attractions.
- Beneath the light surface, the main plot may be rigorous or painful.
- When others tease a character, that character does not retaliate harshly but indulges them, creating a “group favorite.”
- The protagonist behaves irreverently but remains capable; the contrast creates appeal.
- Even lightly absurd comedy can succeed. Creating jokes through dialogue is the essential skill.

### Positioning by Female-Audience Platform

| Platform | Positioning | Writing Calibration |
|------|------|------|
| Jinjiang | High-quality female-audience fiction | High standards for reassuring copy, agency, and character detail |
| Qidian Female | Stable long-form fiction | Use cautiously for highly commercial short fiction; validate the target readership |
| Tomato Novel | Broad mass-market acquisition | Strong hooks, strong emotion, fast feedback |
| Qimao | High-emotion female-audience fiction | Alternate sweetness and pain, use extreme push-pull tension, and allow absurdist comedy or fantasy |

### Safety Rules for Synopsis Copy

- If the synopsis contains no indication of the heroine’s success, readers lack reassurance and acquisition and retention risk rises.
- Too many overlapping mystery hooks make the synopsis difficult to understand.
- A mismatch between title and synopsis repels readers.
- Excessive abuse of the heroine makes the story feel overwhelmingly painful and raises acquisition and retention risk.
- If the career arc is prominent, the synopsis must imply career success.

### Critical Writing Insights

- Reference priority: Comparable-book analysis > theoretical framework.
- Analyze at least three comparable books before identifying their core characteristics.
- For reader-feedback analysis, extract recurring demand keywords from recent comments on the target platform or from scan/analyze reports, then use them as calibration criteria.
- Distant plot events remain broad in the high-level outline; near-term events become specific in the detailed outline.
- Style serves emotion. The key is satisfying the target readers’ emotional needs precisely.

## Nine Ways to Write Shock Scenes

| Type | Technique | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Ordinary shock | Event + one person reacts | A supporting character directly reacts after witnessing the protagonist’s strength |
| Escalating group | A reacts → B reacts, with B ranking above A | A minor figure reacts first, then a major figure reacts more strongly |
| Commentary | Explanatory narration + reaction | Establish the elegance of the strike, then show an observer’s response |
| Visual spectacle | Grand image + reaction | Spectators kneel after a world-changing visual effect |
| Comparison | Character compares the subject with its prior state | “Is this really the same demon ape from moments ago?” |
| Rhetorical question | Self-question and answer followed by shock | “Could I do that? Impossible!” |
| Changing locations | Locations 1 → 2 → 3 react in sequence | News spreads and shocks multiple factions |
| Physical and mental layers | Bodily response + inner response | “Cold throughout the body” + “shaken to the soul” |
| Character contrast | Disdain first, shock later | “That’s nothing” → “I must be dreaming” |

**Core principle**: The satisfaction of shock comes from contrast. First establish the character’s existing belief—contempt or confidence—then break it.

### Three Turns and Four Shocks

```
Turn 1: Protagonist announces a choice → everyone reacts
Turn 2: Specific, unexpected choice revealed → everyone reacts again
Turn 3: Question about the reason / deeper reversal → stronger reaction
Final shock: Highest authority delivers the strongest judgment → rejected candidate leaves dejectedly, briefly lowering the emotion
```

Every turn must exceed the previous level of shock, and each reversal must add new information.

## Prerequisites for Competition Scenes

Before writing a competition or confrontation, disclose:

1. **Stakes/cost**: What is gained or lost.
2. **Relative strength**: At least imply each side’s advantages and disadvantages.
3. **A path to victory**: Readers must see at least one possible route for the protagonist.
4. **Opponent’s distinctiveness**: Why this confrontation deserves space.

### Outcome Design

| Pattern | Difficulty | Effect | Key Point |
|------|------|------|------|
| Strong defeats weak | Low | Best return on effort | Use an information gap for contrast and combine with status display |
| Evenly matched | Medium | Strong tension | Use once in a medium-length story, at the major climax |
| Weak defeats strong | High | Strongest payoff but greatest risk | Requires a credible explanation; do not make opponents irrational |

## Four-Stage Romance Progression

```
Familiarity → Testing → Ambiguity → Commitment
```

| Stage | Status | Core Approach | Prohibition |
|------|------|----------|------|
| Familiarity | Friends/strangers | Establish characterization and first impressions | No behavior reserved for lovers |
| Testing | Interested but uncertain | Testing behavior, misunderstandings, and sweetness | No direct confession |
| Ambiguity | More than friends, less than lovers | Occasional romantic behavior and visible attraction | Female-audience fiction must extend this stage |
| Commitment | Official couple | Romantic affection + greatest obstacle | Do not return to testing |

**Hard rule**: Characters must act according to their current stage. Skipping stages makes the relationship feel sleazy.

### Eight CP Behavior Types—Only After Sufficient Affection

| Behavior Type | Required Affection |
|----------|-----------|
| Intimate behavior, including physical contact | High |
| Claiming behavior, including jealousy and declarations | Medium-high |
| Trust behavior, including unconditional trust and shared secrets | Medium or higher |
| Protective loyalty, defending the other person even after a mistake | Medium or higher |
| Special treatment unlike treatment of anyone else | Medium |
| Comforting behavior, noticing and soothing distress | Medium or higher |
| Priority privilege, making the other person the most important factor | Medium-high |
| Intuitive coordination, acting alike without planning | High |

Affection must accumulate through plot events; it cannot leap without cause.

## Comedy Flag Mechanism

| Character | Flag Rule |
|------|-----------|
| Protagonist | The flag does not come true, or comes true humorously; without humor it becomes toxic |
| Antagonist | The flag must come true—the boast must lead to humiliation |
| Others | The flag usually comes true |

An antagonist’s flag previews a payoff. A protagonist’s flag creates comic tension: “I will never...” becomes funny when immediately overturned.

## Short-Form Viewpoint Rules

### Controlling the Cast

- Open with one principal character interacting with one or two others. Keep the scene small rather than crowded.
- In one-to-many scenes, reduce supporting characters’ presence according to importance.
- Do not introduce everyone at once.
- Focus supporting characters’ attention and internal responses on the protagonist.

### Controlling the Number of Viewpoints

- Default to a first-person victim who dominates the entire experience, as in most short fiction on Tomato Novel, Qimao, and Heiyan. Use other viewpoints only as brief inserts that sharpen regret or triumph.
- Mystery and other exceptions may use two or three viewpoints, never more than three.
- More than three disconnected viewpoints will probably reduce continued reading.
- Use an enemy viewpoint if it gives readers clarity; omit it if readers should speculate.
- Generally use only one enemy viewpoint, occupying one of the three available viewpoint slots.

## Three Emotional Layers and Triggers for Resonance

### Separating the Three Layers

| Layer | Explanation |
|------|------|
| Character emotion | What the character experiences |
| Emotion conveyed by the text | What the text intends readers to receive |
| Emotion experienced by the reader | What readers actually feel |

Do not conflate the three. In one scene, the character may cry, the text may flirt, and the reader may feel exhilarated.

### Emotional Anchor

**Definition**: A concrete beat that activates a reader’s inner resonance. Emotion must touch readers through an anchor; it cannot be produced by piling up direct emotional description.

**Characteristics**: Drawn from experiences or feelings common in real life. Extensive description is unnecessary; a few critical details are enough.

**Examples**:

| Intended Emotion | Weak Approach | Strong Anchor |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Great maternal love | Mother cooks and cleans every day | Mother secretly bows to every classmate and asks them to treat her blind child kindly |
| Survival is difficult | Protagonist digs roots and eats bark | Everyone bids the price down; the protagonist raises a hand and says, “I don’t want money. I only want one meal.” |
| Deep teacher-student bond | Teacher teaches conscientiously | Teacher gives the student the only padded coat and walks through the snow in thin clothes |

### Put the Best Material at the Critical Point

1. Develop the largest plot movement first: greatest conflict → medium conflict → small conflict.
2. Place the strongest emotional anchor at the climax of the large movement.
3. Other portions may remain quieter.
4. Emotional setup is plot progression.

**Design path**: Conflict → emotional requirement → character/background/setting → concrete beat

## A New Approach to Antagonist Mockery

| Dimension | Traditional Approach | New Approach |
|------|----------|--------|
| Purpose | Strike the protagonist’s vulnerability and create frustration | Reveal the antagonist’s ignorance and invite readers to enjoy the joke |
| Reader response | Feel wronged on the protagonist’s behalf | Await the reversal with amusement |
| Protagonist response | Struggle to invent a counterattack | Remain relaxed and unconcerned |

**Procedure**: Identify the protagonist’s advantage, then have the antagonist mock that exact advantage relentlessly. The more the antagonist mocks it, the more clearly readers recognize the antagonist’s ignorance.

**Preserving genuine provocation**: Redirect it toward the protagonist’s friends or family. Let the antagonist attack their weaknesses to provoke anger.

## Begin Character Analysis with Flaws

- Positive character = virtues obscure flaws.
- Negative character = flaws obscure virtues.
- Critical element: What injustice does the character resent? It must resonate with readers.

**Enriching characterization**: Consider decisiveness in violence. Its positive expression is clean efficiency; its negative expression is a lack of humanity. Strengthen or soften “human warmth” to distinguish protagonists and supporting characters. A vivid character contains both strengths and weaknesses.

## Four Elements of the Risk-Little-for-Much Module

1. **A method unique to the protagonist**: Why can this character take the gamble?
2. **The struggle itself**: The process matters more than the result.
3. **A sense of extraordinary profit**: The return greatly exceeds expectations.
4. **A clear objective**: The protagonist must know what they are pursuing.

---

## The Nature of Payoffs and the Three Cultivation Realms

**The essence of a payoff** = changing interpersonal relationships.

The three great cultivation realms are fundamentally: insect → peer → senior. The protagonist’s constant progress creates an imbalance with small plot events and produces satisfaction. Small plots create small payoffs; large plots create large payoffs.

## Quality Check

Use this checklist while writing:

- [ ] Does the opening contain an information bomb within the first 100 Chinese characters rather than scenery or weather?
- [ ] What does each scene change: risk, information, relationship, resources, decision, action, or reader understanding?
- [ ] Does emotion reach readers through an anchor rather than direct description?
- [ ] Do shock scenes establish contrast by creating a belief before breaking it?
- [ ] Does the romance progress without skipping familiarity → testing → ambiguity → commitment?
- [ ] Does every antagonist flag pay off, while protagonist flags either fail or pay off humorously?
- [ ] Are viewpoints limited to two or three, never more than three?
- [ ] Does dialogue change information, strategy, power, relationships, or action? Do scenes without dialogue have another clear progression engine?
- [ ] Does the ending close through action or dialogue without elevation, summary, or reflection?
- [ ] Is there no passage that withholds a deserved payoff, and are the special advantage’s effects clear?
