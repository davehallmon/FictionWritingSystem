## Section 1: Protagonist Card

### Fill-in Template

```
姓名：
性别：
角色定位：（一句话说清主角在故事中的功能）
身份标签：（如：废柴大学生、前特种兵、落魄皇子）
外貌特征：（3-5 个关键词，要有记忆点）
  例：瘦高、总穿旧夹克、左手有疤、眼神懒散
性格关键词：（3-5 个，必须有矛盾面）
  例：嘴毒心软、看似冷漠实则护短、冲动但有底线
核心目标：（全书终点想达成什么）
核心动机：（必须达成的理由——情感驱动，非理性驱动）
致命弱点：（让角色犯错的性格缺陷，非能力缺陷）
口头禅/标志动作：（让读者秒认的标签）
```

### Design Essentials

- Motivation must be emotional. “Avenge his mother” is stronger than “become the strongest.”
- A flaw must cause the protagonist to make mistakes at decisive moments; otherwise, it is not a flaw.
- Appearance and catchphrases are memory anchors and must be specified.
- Names must fit the era, region, and culture. Avoid modernized or politicized names that conflict with the setting. In fan fiction or an established world, retain official names/translations for canonical characters. Name new characters in the style of the source region and do not mix language families within one region.

## Section 2: Supporting-Character Card

### Fill-in Template

```
姓名：
性别：
角色功能：（导师/盟友/情报源/牺牲品/镜像对照）
与主角关系：
核心特质：（1-2 个关键词）
标志性特征：（一句话让读者记住）
退场方式：（何时/如何退出故事）
```

### Design Essentials

- Every supporting character needs a clear function. Do not introduce a character with no function.
- Plan exits deliberately; do not forget characters as the story progresses.
- No more than three supporting characters should have dialogue in one scene.
- Names must follow the setting's era, region, and culture. In fan fiction or an established world, minor and new supporting characters must follow the source's regional naming style. Do not mix language families or insert modernized or politicized names that do not fit.

## Section 3: Antagonist Design

### 3.1 Antagonist Tiers

Choose the tier that matches the antagonist's page time and meet its requirements.

#### Minor Antagonist (1–5 Chapters)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Obstacle in one short arc |
| Design | One or two vivid traits are enough: arrogant, greedy, bullies the weak |
| Exit | Defeated or exposed cleanly and quickly |

#### Mid-Tier Antagonist (10–30 Chapters)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Primary opponent for one volume |
| Motivation | A reason to oppose the protagonist other than “pure evil” |
| Methods | Force/politics/resources |
| Presence | Defeats the protagonist at least once and makes readers furious |
| Exit | Defeated head-on by the protagonist, delivering satisfaction |

#### Major-Arc Boss (One or More Volumes)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Embodies the central conflict of a stage |
| Arc | Complete character arc, personal goal, and internal logic |
| Conflict | Ideological conflict with the protagonist, not merely competing interests |
| Confrontation | At least one encounter that drives the protagonist to desperation |
| Other side | One aspect that makes readers unable to hate them completely |
| Exit | A ceremonial final battle and a resonant ending |

#### Final Boss (Entire Book)

| Dimension | Requirement |
|------|------|
| Function | Personifies the book's central conflict |
| Foreshadowing | Present from Chapter 1 |
| Opposition | Directly opposes the protagonist's goal/motivation and represents the thematic opposite |
| Power | Overwhelms the protagonist, who must transform to win |
| Conviction | Holds a belief; not merely a madman or monster |

### 3.2 Iron Rules for Antagonists

- An antagonist's intelligence and power determine the value of the protagonist's victory. A weak antagonist makes victory meaningless.
- The antagonist's behavior needs internal logic that makes sense from their viewpoint.
- Never make an antagonist stupid just to hand the protagonist a win.

### 3.3 Four Essentials for Establishing an Antagonist

Follow these in order; none may be omitted:

1. **Demonstrate power**: show ability or methods at the entrance.
2. **Credible motivation**: behavior makes sense from the antagonist's viewpoint.
3. **Real threat**: defeat the protagonist at least once.
4. **Timing of ultimate intent**: reserve the true purpose for a decisive reversal.

### 3.4 Four Steps for Establishing an Antagonist's Personality

| Step | Action |
|------|------|
| 1. The antagonist has a dream | In their own eyes, they are the protagonist of their story |
| 2. Unearth a traumatic past | Who raised them? Who loved them? Who hurt them? What pain do they avoid? |
| 3. Hone the character flaw | Under adversity, do not activate positive traits; sharpen the flaw. The “strength” itself becomes the fatal weakness |
| 4. Make the antagonist a mirror | Their strengths reflect the protagonist's weaknesses. Complete opposites create friction every time they meet |

### 3.5 Giving the Antagonist Weight

- Obstacles should arise from characterization, not from the writer arbitrarily obstructing the protagonist.
- Formula: clear motivation → motivation drives behavior → behavior creates obstacles → the antagonist's weight determines obstacle strength.
- When the antagonist is exceptionally dominant, the protagonist's solution should feel sophisticated, intelligent, and unexpected.

## Section 4: Motivation Design

### 4.1 Motivation-Chain Template

```
起因（Cause）    → 角色经历了什么
意图（Intent）   → 角色想做什么
约束（Constraint）→ 什么在阻碍他
风险（Risk）     → 失败的代价是什么
```

**Example**:

```
起因：母亲被仇家杀害
意图：找到凶手并复仇
约束：凶手是当朝权臣，主角只是平民
风险：复仇失败会被灭门，复仇成功会失去所有现有生活
```

### 4.2 Motivation-Chain Essentials

- The cause must be specific. “Bullied” is insufficient; “slapped in front of a crowd” works.
- The constraint must be powerful, or an effortless sweep creates no tension.
- The risk must be real; readers need to believe the protagonist could lose something important.
- Motivation may evolve with the plot, but it cannot change without preparation.

### 4.3 Types of Motivational Conflict

When a character needs an internal struggle, choose one type:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Goal vs. morality | Conflict between what they want and what they should do | Seek revenge without harming innocents |
| Emotion vs. interest | Struggle between feeling and reason | Know someone is unreliable but cannot let go |
| Short term vs. long term | Immediate benefit conflicts with a distant goal | Compromise to survive vs. uphold a principle and gamble on the future |
| Self vs. others | Personal needs conflict with others' expectations | Want a quiet life but carry a family mission |

## Section 5: Characterization Quick Reference

### 5.1 Core Principles

**Three requirements for protagonist behavior**:

1. **Understandable**: readers understand why the protagonist acts.
2. **Relatable**: readers can identify with the protagonist's thinking.
3. **Acceptable**: readers can accept the protagonist's values.

**Show rather than tell**:

- Purpose → show it through behavior, not narratorial explanation.
- Action → use concrete acts and decisions, not stacked adjectives.
- Attitude → reveal it through dialogue and response, not interior monologue.

### 5.2 Ways to Reveal Traits

| Method | Action | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Sensitivity | Touch a weak point and observe the response | Panicking, fleeing, and redirecting attention reveal different personalities |
| Avoidance | Use evasive behavior to imply weakness | What a character refuses to do can reveal more than what they do |
| Strength before flaw | Show an appealing quality before revealing the defect | Readers must like the character before they will tolerate the flaw |

### 5.3 Four Personality Attributes

| Attribute | Definition | Design point |
|---------|------|---------|
| Moral | Beliefs about right and wrong | Influences the development of other traits |
| Achievement | Drives pursuit of goals | Aligns with morality and serves the goal |
| Interactive | Style of dealing with people | The largest set of positive traits |
| Identity | Sense of who they are | A basic component of personality |

Choose one as the primary trait and use the others in support.

### 5.4 Four-Layer Characterization Method

| Layer | Method |
|------|------|
| Empathy | Avoid too many opening characters; let readers know the protagonist and form expectations first |
| Minimum trait (label) | Build the protagonist from inside out; build supporting characters from outside in through labels |
| Insincerity | Speech ≠ thought and action ≠ attitude. Everyday life and a character's death are opposite poles for deepening character |
| Spotlight | Supporting characters need a strong connection to the protagonist. If one eclipses the protagonist, introduce another supporting character who draws away that spotlight |

### 5.5 Presented Character vs. Character Truth

| Level | Definition | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Presentation (explicit) | Trait readers see first | A youthful, cute petite girl |
| Character truth (implicit) | The person's genuine inner nature | Actually a cold-blooded killer |
| Value orientation (deep) | Values that decide a critical choice | Chooses ambition over justice |

Do not reveal value orientation immediately; let a decisive choice expose it naturally. The later the character truth is revealed, the greater the dramatic tension.

### 5.6 Layered Character Revelation

| Layer | Action |
|------|------|
| First | Tell readers the archetype: petite cutie/mature beauty/young wife |
| Second | Add distinctive labels: curvy, long-legged, tan skin |
| Third | Give the personality a cause: family or inner drive |
| Fourth | Break the pattern: the adorable airhead actually understands everything, instantly deepening the characterization |

Strengthen memory without fearing repetition. What seems simplistic may actually reinforce recall.

### 5.7 Three-Timeline Method for Character Experience

| Timeline | Content |
|--------|------|
| Past | Background, upheaval, origins of personality |
| Present | Relationship to the protagonist and current interaction pattern |
| Future | Motivation or crisis that drives growth, such as a kidnapped teacher, captured heroine, or antagonist's revelation |

### 5.8 Obsession Method for Supporting Characters

Quickly earn reader investment in a subplot character:

- Give the character one obsession; organize behavior, interests, and personality around it.
- It need not appear every time. Avoid contradicting it, then reveal it at a key moment.
- If the obsession is extreme, add an approachable everyday detail to make the character relatable.
- Use this for subplot characters, not major supporting characters who appear constantly.

### 5.9 Recurring Character Behaviors

A key method for keeping long-form fiction consistent:

- Choose one behavioral trait and repeat it.
- Method: determine what type readers like → turn it into behavior → repeat it in different scenes.
- Character attraction + core attraction = layering; cycling both creates differentiation.
- Antagonists and supporting characters also need recurring attractions. A lovable antagonist increases freshness.

### 5.10 Write Behavior Around Characterization

Behavior, language, and thought must all grow from personality. If the plot requires an out-of-character act, revise the plot first; do not rewrite the personality.

### 5.11 Character Drives Event vs. Event Drives Character

| Driver | Characteristics |
|---------|------|
| Character drives event | Plot naturally follows personality, motivation, and choice, deepening the arc |
| Event drives character | An external event breaks equilibrium and forces the true self into view |

When stuck, seek direction in character motivation rather than inventing arbitrary plot.

## Section 6: Protagonist-Specific Issues

### 6.1 Protagonist Presence

- The protagonist must command respect; this is the basis of reader identification.
- Avoid excessive foolish interior monologue and profanity.
- The protagonist may be imperfect but must not inspire contempt.

### 6.2 Protagonist Growth

- Growth appears in judgment and choices, not only power.
- Every development needs a triggering event and internal reflection.
- Growth is optional in web fiction. A psychologically complete, decent person at the opening can be easier to identify with; stable characterization has its own appeal.

### 6.3 Protagonist Red Lines

Never write these protagonist types:

| Prohibited | Reason |
|------|------|
| Self-righteous do-gooder | Readers want satisfaction, not moral lectures |
| Mindless combat machine | Difficult to connect with emotionally |
| Fundamentally evil | Readers cannot identify with the character |
| Makes mistakes through stupidity or sanctimony | Readers will not forgive them |
| Gives up on themselves | Readers dislike this more than seeing them bullied |

### 6.4 When the Protagonist Starts Too High

**Warning signs**:

- No room to grow because ability, position, or resources have reached the ceiling
- No dramatic tension; plot becomes a repetitive effortless slaughter
- Readers lose motivation to continue

**Solutions**:

- **Reduce pressure, not dignity**: lower the scale of momentum and expectation without undermining ability or self-respect.
- **Preserve dimensional gaps**: even if exceptionally strong in one dimension, keep weaknesses in others.
- **Excess-gain method**: an apparent windfall becomes even larger after the protagonist calculates its true value.

### 6.5 Establishing the Protagonist in the Opening

- Characterization is established through choices; the opening must make the protagonist choose.
- An opening built on strategy establishes character more easily than one built on violence.
- Readers identify more readily with “smart but ordinary” than “invincible immediately.”

### 6.6 Two Identities and Two Cheat Abilities

| Dimension | Definition | Function |
|------|------|------|
| Explicit identity | Social identity/profession + status | Concentrates early conflicts and must continually evolve or change |
| Hidden identity | Extraordinary origin | Concentrates mid-to-late conflicts |
| Explicit cheat ability | System/jade pendant/rebirth memory | Acquired at the opening and carried throughout |
| Hidden cheat ability | Personality | What truly distinguishes the protagonist |

**Best approach**: use the explicit cheat ability for early displays, then clarify personality as the plot advances.  
**Characterization and the book's overall feel**: the protagonist must fit the world's tone. Align social identity + origin + cheat ability + personality.

### 6.7 Deliberately Simple Protagonist + Animal Companions

If the protagonist is intentionally given a simple personality, readers have fewer reasons to reject them. Fewer actions mean fewer mistakes and maximum identification.

**Problem**: an overly simple personality reduces excitement and makes the story difficult to move.  
**Solution**: give an aloof protagonist three to five animal companions with distinct personalities.

| Companion type | Function |
|---------|------|
| Goofy and cute | Supplies comedy; the protagonist should not be humiliated, but the animal can be |
| Clever and sharp-tongued | Speaks what the protagonist should not say and preserves their stature |
| Frank and dim-witted | Creates a lovable contrast |
| Timid with an unusual power | Supplements abilities |

### 6.8 Balancing Core Emotion and Self-Expression

- The underlying need stays constant; only the form changes.
- After writing a plot segment, review it from the reader's viewpoint for drift from the core hook.
- When the protagonist becomes powerful, they must gain something; do not make them only pay costs.
- Strong self-expression creates the greatest drift risk, so check it proactively.

### 6.9 Reduce Pressure, Not Dignity: Correct Sources of Conflict

**Wrong**: an antagonist targets the protagonist for no credible reason.

**Right**: the antagonist pursues a personal objective that objectively creates the protagonist's predicament without deliberately targeting them as an individual.

**Conflict chain**: antagonist's objective → suppresses the group containing the protagonist (reduced pressure) → protagonist overturns it → the lower class remains numb → protagonist changes approach → a new conflict arises naturally.

Core principle: everyone is driven by desired interests. Underneath are different values, deeper still are different formative environments, and deepest is the worldview.

## Section 7: Deepening Antagonists

### 7.1 Core Principles

- A classic antagonist has a complete worldview and internally consistent values.
- The same external event changes different people differently; external and internal causes resonate.
- An antagonist cannot be built only from external events without inner values and logic.

### 7.2 Write the Antagonist Like a Protagonist

**Traditional problem**: after a map change, the old antagonist cannot keep pace, so a new cardboard antagonist appears.

**Solution**:

- Write the antagonist as if they were a protagonist.
- Add “supporting characters elevate one another” to “supporting characters elevate the protagonist.”
- During a map change, use the new map to prove the old character's value, not the old character's helplessness to prove the new map's difficulty.

## Quality Checklist

After character design, check every item:

### Protagonist

- [ ] Motivation is emotional, not an empty statement such as “become the strongest”
- [ ] Has a fatal flaw that causes mistakes at critical moments
- [ ] Appearance has 3–5 memorable keywords
- [ ] Has a catchphrase or signature action
- [ ] Maintains presence and does not invite contempt
- [ ] Crosses none of the red lines: sanctimony, mindless violence, inner evil, stupidity-driven mistakes, self-abandonment
- [ ] Core objective aligns with the book's main plot

### Supporting Characters

- [ ] Has a clear function: mentor/ally/information source/sacrifice/mirror
- [ ] Core traits fit one or two keywords
- [ ] Has a memorable signature feature
- [ ] Exit is planned
- [ ] No more than three supporting characters speak in one scene

### Antagonists

- [ ] Designed according to the tier table; page time matches tier
- [ ] Entrance demonstrates power or methods
- [ ] Motivation makes sense from the antagonist's viewpoint
- [ ] Defeats the protagonist at least once at mid tier or above
- [ ] Is not made stupid to hand over a win
- [ ] Major-arc boss has an ideological conflict, not merely competing interests
- [ ] Final boss is foreshadowed from Chapter 1

### Motivation

- [ ] Cause is specific, not vague language such as “was bullied”
- [ ] Constraint is strong; the protagonist cannot sweep through effortlessly
- [ ] Risk is real; readers believe something important can be lost
- [ ] Evolution is prepared and never arbitrary

### Characterization

- [ ] Purpose is shown through behavior, not narratorial explanation
- [ ] Behavior/language/thought follow characterization; plot never rewrites personality for convenience
- [ ] Major characters have recurring behavior points that preserve long-form consistency
- [ ] Supporting characters connect clearly to the protagonist without stealing the spotlight
- [ ] Character truth/value orientation emerges naturally at decisive choices
