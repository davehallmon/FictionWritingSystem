# Source-Text Character Relationship Analysis Criteria

## Use as Deconstruction Criteria

This file began as a writing-side playbook phrased as “how to write.” During deconstruction, `story-short-analyze` uses it as a
**comparative benchmark**: compare the source text with the standard patterns described here to identify which one it uses and how effectively it uses it. **Do not** use this file to write a new work during analysis.

From a reader’s perspective, these are industrial conventions for popular short fiction such as Fanqie short stories, Story Club tales, wife-chasing redemption, social-condition stories, rebirth, and revenge against abusers. They are the comparative source for “reusable structure / reversal design / genre formulas” and similar report sections.

## Character Relationship Types

Use the table to classify the relationship, then apply the principles.

| Relationship | Definition | Function | Example |
|---------|------|------|------|
| Conflict | Opposed interests or beliefs | Creates tension and advances plot | Nemeses, competitors |
| Alliance | Shared objective | Provides assistance and creates bonds | Comrades, mentor and student |
| Intimacy | Emotional bond | Creates vulnerability and emotional support | Lovers, family, siblings |
| Authority | Superior/subordinate or controlling relationship | Creates pressure and limits action | Master, employer, regulator |

**Rules**:
- Test every important relationship at least once through betrayal, sacrifice, or misunderstanding.
- Every relationship needs a changing arc: enemy to ally, ally to opponent.
- Do not make every relationship uniformly harmonious.
- A relationship must serve the plot, not exist only to create sweetness or pain.

## Core Characterization for Romance-Driven Stories

In romance-driven fiction, plot serves characterization. Every event must enrich a character or advance the relationship; otherwise it is ineffective.

### Construction Steps

1. **Define the characterization core**: write the first defining trait that came to mind, such as “a prince who wants the throne” or “a sickly prince.”
2. **Expand around the core**: ask why it exists, what happened before, how environment shaped it, and what caused the personality.
3. **Extend the plot line**: write the plot inherent in the core—goal creates a goal line; pain creates a healing line.

### Rules

- A familiar plot paired with full characterization can escape a formulaic feeling.
- Both protagonists need strengths and independent trajectories of change; one cannot shine while the other fades.
- A highlight is not merely showing off. Any moment that leaves a deep impression—including devastating pain—is a highlight.
- Life is more than romance. A character may want love, but a life containing only love is thin and cannot stand.

## Underlying Differences Between Male- and Female-Oriented Romance

**Identify the target readership first, then use its logic. Mixing them makes the story feel wrong to readers.**

### Male-Oriented Romance Logic

| Dimension | Description |
|------|------|
| Core | “Displaying external excellence”—the couple demonstrates how exceptional both people are |
| Three core steps | “If only she were mine” → “this exceptional person is mine” → “someone this exceptional loves me, so I am even more exceptional” |
| Bystander response | “I envy him for winning such an exceptional woman,” not “their love is so sweet” |
| Heroines | Prefer several women to admire the protagonist; more admirers signal greater excellence |
| Essential role | A “trophy”; the ultimate motivation is victory |

Design male-oriented romantic events around “demonstrating excellence.”

### Female-Oriented Romance Logic

| Dimension | Description |
|------|------|
| Core | “Connection”—a unique, unbreakable, highest-priority emotional bond |
| Requirement | “I love you as a person; looks, wealth, and talent cannot replace this connection” |
| Purity | As the bond forms, external factors should progressively lose influence |
| Both parties | Ideally first love and mutually exclusive devotion, protecting uniqueness and purity |
| Bystander response | “Their love is so sweet; I envy them” |

Design female-oriented romantic events around “deepening connection.”

## Role Selection for Transmigration into a Book or Game

### Required Conditions

- The narrative protagonist must know the original world well; this is the primary source of anticipation.
- Establish the familiar world in one or two sentences. Do not spend more than a chapter without knowing where the protagonist is.
- Select a post-transmigration role with strong drama or conflict, such as the vicious female supporting character.

### Information-Gap Design

- Add an appropriate information gap to the original world, giving the protagonist room to explore.
- Let knowledge of the original world exploit a bug for outsized returns.

## Rivalry-Scene Resolution Strategies

A romantic rivalry scene is fundamentally a conflict. Control its intensity; do not create irreconcilable conflict among romantic interests.

| Resolution | Operation |
|---------|------|
| Reconnect to the career line | Convert jealousy into competition over who contributes more to the protagonist’s career |
| Insert a career-line emergency | At peak confrontation, make everyone shift from infighting to a common external threat |
| Pass over briefly | At most, two people dislike each other and exchange a couple of barbs; remain restrained |

## Avoiding Traps in a Sweet-but-Naïve Character

### Six Traps to Avoid

1. The heroine’s mistake cannot be deliberate or knowingly forbidden.
2. Her mistake cannot violate morality; childlike innocence with high moral standards is the type’s defining trait.
3. She cannot condemn others from moral high ground while failing to meet the standard herself.
4. After her moral grandstanding causes conflict, do not force teammates to apologize and praise her.
5. Correct pattern: higher moral standard → conduct matches it → the consistency makes her distinctive and slightly foolish.
6. A growth line may put her in the condemned person’s position, where she discovers that the earlier choice was already the best available.

### Inverse Use

To create a sweet-but-naïve antagonist, give her all six faults so readers immediately dislike her.

## Two-Way Reversal of Characterization

A method for adjusting characterization when a romantic relationship changes.

### Core Idea

- The strongest change transforms both people, with each becoming the inverse of their earlier relational role.
- Base change on localized adjustments to the original characterization; keep it tightly connected and avoid arbitrary reconstruction.

### Rules

- After the “love divided by mountains and seas” phase—especially after the greatest obstacle—do not design a new conflict. Deliver romantic payoff.
- The real obstacle is already resolved; readers most urgently want sweetness.
- Express love through concrete couple behavior and attentive care, not manufactured “I love you” sweetness.

## Character Behavioral-Coherence Check

### Step 1: Exclude External Plot Force

Check whether the character acts only to move the plot in a predetermined direction. If the character “happens” to advance the plot without a personal motive, mark “characterization drift” and add a character-level reason.

### Step 2: Confirm the Emotional Objective

Before each passage, define three elements:
1. Target emotion: what should readers feel?
2. Does personality support that emotion naturally?
3. Does behavior fit the character’s functional mission in this passage?

### Step 3: Derive Behavior from Characterization

Derive scene behavior from established experience, personality, objective, and current state:
- List two or three possible reactions.
- Evaluate consistency with existing behavior patterns.
- Select the response most faithful to characterization and richest in dramatic tension.

### Step 4: Verify Emotional Consistency

Compare the core emotion expressed by the character with the scene’s target emotion. If they differ, adjust behavior or the scene objective until the emotional dimension is coherent.

**Two execution paths**:
- Path A: define target emotion → derive behavior from characterization → verify consistency.
- Path B: derive behavior from characterization → infer the scene’s target emotion → verify consistency.

## Supporting-Character Conversion Buffer

A supporting character’s changing attitude toward the protagonist is the process of the protagonist winning them over, carrying strong anticipation and payoff.

### Buffer Types

Online vs. offline, comments made behind someone’s back, living apart, differences in status, intimacy, trust, and information.

### Steps

1. Always preserve a buffer.
2. Select five to seven event turning points in the volume outline.
3. At each, record the supporting character’s state and attitude change.
4. At every major conversion point, make the attitude change explicit.
5. The attitude change itself produces emotion and anticipation.

### Rules

- Supporting characters cannot stand like NPCs waiting for the protagonist to trigger them.
- Give supporting characters independent actions and let their viewpoint generate events.
- This also applies to positive characters: allies need their own actions and motives.

## Create Conflict Through Differences in Perceived Identity

One character’s “reputation” changes dynamically across observers; it is not constant.

| Perspective | Priority | Evaluation of male protagonist |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Conventional | Equal family standing and capacity to withstand risk | Overall compatibility |
| Male protagonist | Providing for a family plus love | Roughly well matched |
| Heroine | Love, exclusivity, care | Caring makes them well matched |
| Rich heir | Looks and family-background compatibility | I am the better match |
| Bystander | Overall comparison | She should marry the rich heir |

**Operational essentials**:
- Different evaluations of the same character naturally create conflict.
- One core payoff in romance is the difference among evaluation dimensions.

## Rival-and-Friend Relationship

The most compelling relationship type.

### Rules

- Both characters must be appealing; otherwise forced equality becomes an irritating attachment.
- Core: profound recognition + absolute conflict → mutual respect. Remove either and the appeal vanishes.
- True nemeses recognize each other; an outsider’s declaration does not count.

## Competitor Positioning and Family-Line Uses

### Competitor: The Catfish Effect

- Positioning: pressure the hero or heroine and create urgency → advance the courtship.
- Place before the climax node as setup.
- It contributes little after the climax and is not a device for indefinitely extending the story.

### Four Directions for a Family Line

| Direction | Function | Timing |
|------|------|---------|
| Male-side support | Provides money, status, or power | Before climax |
| Male-side resistance | Sets obstacles so the protagonist must earn recognition | Before and after a node |
| Female-side support | A warm emotional refuge | Before climax |
| Female-side resistance | Parents disapprove → protagonist strives for recognition | Before and after a node |

Support is mainly useful before the climax. Resistance works around nodes and is generally more versatile.

## Male-Oriented Romance Writing Method

### Why Readers Choose Romance

| Value | Source |
|------|------|
| Emotional | Fills the need to be wanted and cared for |
| Self-esteem | Exceptional heroine → pride in being seen with her → envy from supporting characters |
| Reason for high-status heroine | Stronger tool for triumphant reversals plus greater self-esteem payoff |

### Core Technique: Write Romance as Progression Fantasy

- Ambiguous romantic tension is the most compelling part.
- Affection progress bar: each advance is a “level” in progression fiction.
- Every event is an “upgrade”: conflict, resolution, understanding, and resonance raise affection.

### Affection Progression Path

Stranger → good person (helps an elderly woman cross the street) → decent and brave person (steps forward in danger) → good friend (understands family hardship) → attraction without recognition (an unusual event exposes private sides of both people).

### Key Principle: The Protagonist Does Not Pursue Actively

- Current male-oriented readers generally dislike a “sycophantic pursuer.”
- Affection advances because the hero’s qualities attract, not because he pursues.
- He helps casually and effortlessly → she remembers him.

### Asymmetry in Romantic Progression

- Maintain two progress lines: surface social relationship (strangers → friends → lovers) and actual emotional affection.
- Do not advance them together. One fast and one slow creates rich conflict.
- Emotion ahead of social status → *Kaguya-sama* pattern: mutual affection is full, but pride prevents confession.
- Social status ahead of emotion → live-in-son-in-law/*Way of Choices* pattern: publicly married, privately no affection.

### Showing the Heroine’s Care

| Heroine type | Mode of giving |
|---------|---------|
| Insecure and socially anxious | Secretly leaves helpful things but fears discovery |
| Tsundere | Makes a careful lunch but insists, “I made extra, so I brought it along” |
| Direct and bold | Gives gifts openly or confesses directly |

### Use Contrast Well

| Contrast type | Operation |
|---------|------|
| Timeline | Her attitude at first meeting vs. after familiarity |
| Double standard | Nobody else may touch her head, but the protagonist may → demonstrates special status |
| Information gap | Dual online/offline identities → anticipation of the reveal reaction |

## Affection System: General Framework

### Four Stages

Chance encounter → romantic comedy → love divided by mountains and seas → ending.

### Affection × Relationship-Stage Matrix

Affection (negative/zero/half/full) × relationship stage (familiar/testing/ambiguous/confirmed).

Matching rule: **use the lower side**. If affection advances but relationship stage does not, behavior feels abrupt.

### Three Thresholds for Couple Behavior

| Behavior type | Threshold | Description |
|---------|------|------|
| Requires tolerance | Half affection+ | Active intimacy or compromise requires compensation or becomes sycophancy |
| Special treatment | Half affection+ | Possessiveness, trust, sacrifice, privilege, reassurance |
| Associative behavior | Both at half affection+ | Rapport, sharing, companionship |

Writing tolerance-requiring behavior with insufficient affection feels greasy or sexually harassing.

### Five Romance-Line Templates

| Template | Core |
|------|------|
| Affection change | May rise/fall/already fell → corresponding path → aftertaste |
| Benefit | Enjoying benefits from a partner is the core triumphant payoff |
| Jealous competition | Multiple characters compete for the protagonist; the protagonist is not jealous of others |
| Blocked development | Obstacle → attempted solution → solution → aftertaste |
| Public affection | Everyday couple interaction |

## Independent Character Goals and Relationship-Line Design

### Protagonist Goal Independence

- The protagonist’s goal must be their own, never merely “help someone else achieve a goal,” or the protagonist becomes a supporting tool.
- Convert another person’s goal into the protagonist’s own: suppress rebellion = protect personal interests/earn recognition/acquire resources.
- Self-check: open a random chapter → what is the protagonist actively pursuing? → if nothing, the protagonist has become someone else’s pawn.

### Layered Romance-Line Design

- Romantic progress is not linear. Use upgrade nodes, each corresponding to a plot climax.
- Misalignment between the social relationship and actual emotional line creates tension.
- Best rhythm: slow first, fast later → accumulate affection through early setup → release it late → match with a grand ceremony.

### Functional Supporting-Character Positioning

| Type | Function | Timing |
|------|------|---------|
| Competitor (catfish) | Pressures the protagonist into action | Before climax node |
| Supportive friend or relative | Provides resources or emotional support | Before climax |
| Resistant friend or relative | Rejects → sets conditions → protagonist overcomes → grants recognition | Before and after nodes |

---

## Manipulative or Negative Characters

- They can advance plot but require caution.
- Verify that the character is irreplaceable.
- When the protagonist is strong, any character can become positive feedback.
- When the protagonist is weak, readers will hate a negative character.

## Quality Checklist

After each relationship or romance-line design, verify:

- [ ] **Relationship type is explicit**: every important relationship is conflict/alliance/intimacy/authority.
- [ ] **Relationship has an arc**: every important relationship undergoes at least one test or change.
- [ ] **Characterization has a core**: the protagonist has a clear core trait and basis for expansion.
- [ ] **Goal independence**: the protagonist pursues a personal goal rather than another person’s.
- [ ] **Affection matches behavior**: couple behavior fits the current affection stage, using the lower side.
- [ ] **Fits the readership**: male-oriented events “demonstrate excellence”; female-oriented events “deepen connection.”
- [ ] **Supporting characters act**: they do not wait like NPCs but pursue their own actions and motives.
- [ ] **Buffer remains**: the conversion process retains a buffer and records attitude changes at turning points.
- [ ] **Rivalry stays controllable**: romantic interests have no irreconcilable conflict.
- [ ] **Sweetness arrives at the right time**: after “love divided by mountains and seas,” add no new conflict; provide concrete couple behavior.
- [ ] **Characters have lives beyond romance**: nobody is merely a thin emotional tool.
