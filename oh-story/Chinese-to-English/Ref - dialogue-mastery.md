# Dialogue Design Operations Manual

> Load this file when writing dialogue scenes. Use the decision guide to select a dialogue mode, then apply the operating rules to control quality.

---

## Decision Guide

| Your dialogue scene involves | Mode | Operation |
|-------------|---------|------|
| Protagonist overwhelms/humiliates an opponent | Suppression | Opponent delivers a long speech → protagonist replies with one word |
| Protagonist reveals a trump card/reversal | Reversal | Opponent acts arrogantly → protagonist states one fact → opponent falls silent |
| Relationship collapse/emotional death | Emotional shutdown | Replies grow shorter: explanation → silence → “whatever” |
| Everyday interaction/characterization | Everyday | Bring other characters into the conflict; do not let the protagonist monologue alone |
| Crowd shock/bullet comments | Crowd commentary | Escalate from ordinary shock → expert analysis → reaction from someone with special status |
| Information/worldbuilding | Embedded information | Wrap facts in the character's voice instead of mechanically stating the setting |
| Emotional push-pull/heartbreak | Emotional progression | Alternate rises and falls like a tug-of-war that increases anticipation |

### Additional Dialogue-Mode Guidance

| Dialogue type | Common problem | Correction |
|----------|---------|---------|
| Question-and-answer throughout, like an interrogation | Let one person speak proactively and the other react through action, expression, or thought |
| Conflict that is too polite | Escalate through five levels: tactful refusal → friendly appeal → commanding denial → manipulative pressure → direct insult |
| Everyday dialogue that becomes filler | Its function is characterization; if others can join the conflict, do not let the protagonist monologue |
| Confusing or empty group dialogue | Before writing, assign each character a function: information source/emotional amplifier/conflict creator |

## Core Dialogue Rules

Every line must do at least one of the following or be deleted:

1. **Advance the plot**: reveal new information or move events forward.
2. **Increase anticipation**: suggest what may happen or create suspense.
3. **Reveal character**: communicate personality through speech patterns.

In an emotional scene, every line must also **respond to the other person's preceding emotional state** by receiving, redirecting, escalating, or retreating. Dialogue is a collision between two emotional states, not alternating information broadcasts. Plot movement without emotional continuity between lines produces mechanical dialogue.

### Absolutely Prohibited

| Prohibited | Reason |
|------|------|
| Supporting characters mindlessly praise the protagonist | Feels false |
| Characters explain information readers already know to one another | Padding |
| Long expository speeches | Dull |
| Every character speaks the same way | Blurs identity |
| Dialogue alone persuades someone | People are rarely convinced by a few lines; use an unexpected event instead |
| Emotional direction changes after the scene is written | Return to the emotional breakdown and revise |

## Power-Struggle Dialogue

### Rule

Dialogue length reflects power. The person in control speaks briefly and calmly; the disadvantaged person speaks more and with greater emotion.

### Suppression Mode

Structure: opponent's long speech (3–5 lines) → protagonist's one-word reply.

```
"你以为你是什么东西？我告诉你，这个家轮不到你说话！你嫁进来的那天起就该明白自己的位置。"
"滚。"
```

### Reversal Mode

Structure: opponent boasts (2–3 lines) → protagonist reveals a trump card (one factual line) → opponent falls silent.

```
"你有什么资格管？这是我家的钱，我想怎么花怎么花。"
"你妈的存折，密码是我的生日。"
```

### Emotional-Shutdown Mode

Structure: replies grow shorter, moving from explanation to silence to `随意` (“whatever”).

```
"你听我解释，那天不是你想的那样。"
"嗯。"
"真的，我可以证明。"
"随意。"
```

### Operating Rules

- When the person in control or the protagonist reveals a trump card: dialogue ≤10 Chinese characters, with no accompanying action.
- For the suppressed person: dialogue ≥20 Chinese characters; actions such as clenching a fist, biting a lip, or standing may be added.
- In a two-person exchange, the short-sentence speaker holds the higher-power position and the long-sentence speaker holds the lower one.

## Subtext and Agendas

### Subtext Rules

- Never state a character's true motivation plainly in dialogue.
- Real people justify themselves; characters should too.
- Design two elements for every line: the character's motive, which they may not recognize, and the excuse they give.

### Dialogue Agendas

- Every character enters with a personal agenda: what do they want from this conversation?
- Tension comes from agendas colliding.
- Matching agendas and positions produce repetition and remove purpose.

### Tone Comes from Three Factors

Relationship × setting × objective = tone

| Setting | Characteristics | Suitable content |
|------|------|----------|
| Private, one-on-one | Deep and emotionally open | Confession, conspiracy, declaration of love |
| Public | Must preserve dignity and restrain wording | Break restraint for impact, such as a public rupture |
| Familiar group, friends/peers | Intermediate depth | Relaxed interaction and information exchange |

Private words spoken publicly carry impact. Use actions, expressions, and environment for anything dialogue cannot fully express.

## Emotion-Driven Dialogue

### Intense Emotional Dialogue

- Commands and denials evoke the strongest emotion: “Haven't I made myself clear?”
- The harshest rhetoric pretends to serve the listener's welfare: every sentence sounds caring while conveying contempt, accusation, and disgust.
- Direct denial hurts more than subtle implication.

### Emotional Continuity

Emotion changes continuously and gradually. Anger becomes happiness through anger → less anger → no anger → happiness, with an event triggering each transition. Never skip steps.

### Four Emotional Steps

All four are required:

1. Encounter an event that creates imbalance.
2. React emotionally through action, expression, or language.
3. Think internally; even “not thinking” should be shown to express recklessness.
4. Act in response based on the first three steps.

### Four Ways to Keep Dialogue Engaging

- Let the dialogue itself create or strengthen a central driver: anticipation, satisfaction, or suspense.
- Obstruct information exchange for a reason that may bring the driver closer.
- Let events suddenly depart from reader expectations while remaining reasonable and consistent with characterization.
- Place long dialogue between an anticipation point and its payoff; readers will tolerate the middle to reach the promised satisfaction.

## Revealing Information and Worldbuilding

- Large amounts of information delivered through dialogue feel verbose. Convert some into plot, interiority, narration, environment, or action.
- Wrap information in each character's voice and position; do not recite setting facts mechanically.
- Introduce only the piece of setting currently needed. Do not explain every cause and consequence.
- **Do not use a character as an “exposition mouthpiece”**: no character, especially an information-focused or AI companion, may explain a full block of setting, principles, or backstory. Gate G applies to dialogue too. Break it into half a sentence forced out under pressure + bodily response + implication, revealing only what the moment needs.

### Information Push-Pull Example: “The Protagonist's New Book Takes Off”

| Character | Line | Function |
|------|------|------|
| A | `听说成绩……` (“I heard the numbers…”) | Suspense that builds anticipation |
| B | `肯定没人看！` (“Nobody's reading it!”) | Downturn + anticipation |
| A | `听说成绩很不错` (“I heard the numbers are strong.”) | Upturn + anticipation |
| B | `首订一万三？` (“Thirteen thousand first-day subscriptions?”) | Reveal central information + deliver payoff |

Alternating upward and downward movement creates a tug-of-war that continually raises anticipation.

## Distinctive Character Voices

Each character needs an individual speaking style. If you repeatedly cannot decide what a character would say, the characterization is vague. Study the speech of similar character types.

| Dimension | Operation |
|------------|------|
| Verbal habits and stock phrases | Give each major character one signature expression |
| Rhythm | Long speeches vs. rapid short lines |
| Information preference | Technical characters use professional terms; people of the martial world use cant |
| Fixed position | A character consistently speaks from one angle: pessimistic/optimistic/pragmatic |
| Identity affects wording | Elder/youth/aristocrat/commoner use different diction, self-reference, honorifics, and humility forms |
| Personality affects tone | Strategists imply more than they state; reckless characters say their first thought; calm characters use precision, making rare emotion more powerful |
| Relationship progress affects attitude | First meeting/familiarity/opposition/intimacy changes tone and information depth |

## Bullet Comments and Crowd Dialogue

Their three core functions are advancing plot by revealing information unknown to the protagonist, increasing anticipation through suspense and speculation, and coloring emotion by spreading crowd shock, excitement, or anger to readers. They enhance the main plot; they do not replace it.

### Designed vs. Undesigned Crowd Commentary

- Undesigned: only `卧槽好厉害` (“Damn, that's amazing”), producing monotonous waste.
- Designed: ordinary-person shock → expert analysis → special-status reaction → emotional elevation.

### Operating Points

- Give commenters distinct voices; entries cannot all sound alike.
- Keep each concise, with no more than one sentence's central information.
- Escalate from initial shock toward recognition of the whole picture.
- Include a reversal commenter whose apparently casual line changes everyone's understanding.
- Do not use crowd commentary every chapter. Concentrate it around key payoffs, thrilling moments, and emotional peaks.

## Pacing Control

### Maintaining Pace During Heavy Dialogue

- Do not delete particles that express personality merely to “tighten” prose.
- Interweave action, environmental change, and thought between dialogue passages.
- Keep dialogue brief under tension; let it lengthen during relief.
- Place key information at the beginning or end, using the middle for emotional push-pull.

### Handling Actions and Expressions

- Attaching an action or expression to every line makes prose mechanical.
- Use them at decisive turns, where they have the greatest effect.
- In low-key scenes such as tea or a walk, use small actions and silence to establish atmosphere.

### Breathing Room

- After several dialogue exchanges, insert environment or interiority to let the scene breathe.
- Pauses created by action, line breaks, and short sentences carry more tension than uninterrupted speech.
- “Are you sure?” creates more pressure than a long explanation.

## Length Control

### When There Is Too Much Dialogue

- Summarize exchanges containing information readers already know in one narrative sentence: “Alice told Anna why she had come to the city. Anna frowned as she listened.”
- Replace dialogue with a sudden event when possible.
- If removing verbal particles makes the exchange lifeless, the dialogue itself lacks information and needs rewriting.

### When There Is Too Little Dialogue

- If another character can state something, do not flatten it into protagonist narration.
- Introduce a supporting character into conflict and conversation, but give every new person a role in the main plot.

## Filling Dialogue with Memorable Bits

### Trope-Based vs. Ordinary

- Ordinary: `"兄弟别灰心，你一步步走到今天我是看着你过来的，这点挫折对于你来说算什么事儿？振作起来！"`
- Trope-based: `"兄弟别灰心，我相信你总有人头落地，落地人头，头落地上……卧槽那句话怎么说的来着？兄弟你懂我意思是吧……"`
- The trope-based version creates humor through “unable to say it correctly, but the meaning lands.”

### Operations

- Integrate memes, bits, or playful lines to increase overall entertainment.
- Use them especially in standout dialogue for the protagonist or major supporting characters.
- A memorable line can become a climax around which the entire sequence is designed.
- **Situational exception; voice yields to tone**: During high-pressure, life-or-death, grief-heavy, or solemn beats, comedy characters and lighthearted supporting characters must restrain jokes, catchphrases, and banter. Their voice yields to the current emotional register; use brief, cold responses with emotional weight. Save jokes for safe or breathing beats. Self-check: would the joke pull readers out of the current tone? If so, delete or revise it.

## Quality Check

### Three Primary Checks

If any one is true, revise:

- [ ] Must large amounts of information be shown through dialogue?
- [ ] Does dialogue follow an interrogation-like question-and-answer pattern?
- [ ] Does the story habitually rely on dialogue to drive every plot or character change?

### Core Directive Checks

- [ ] Power struggle: controller ≤10 Chinese characters / suppressed person ≥20; is the suppression, reversal, or emotional-shutdown mode clear?
- [ ] Subtext and agenda: each participant has an agenda, and true motivation remains outside the spoken line.
- [ ] Voice differentiation: with names hidden, can you identify the speaker through all seven dimensions?
- [ ] Crowd progression: ordinary → expert → special status; does it have layers?
- [ ] Plot movement: at the end of each exchange, has the plot advanced?
- [ ] Length control: does any single exchange remain under 40% of the section, with sufficient information density?

### Testing Dialogue Quality

- Read every line for natural spoken exchange rather than formal question-and-answer prose.
- Check whether the end of the conversation foreshadows the coming shift in pace.
