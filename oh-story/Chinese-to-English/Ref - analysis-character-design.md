# Source-Text Character Design Analysis Criteria

> Purpose: an instruction set for character design methods. Use the decision route to find the applicable method, then execute it step by step.

## Use as Deconstruction Criteria

This file began as a writing-side playbook phrased as “how to write.” During deconstruction, `story-short-analyze` uses it as a
**comparative benchmark**: compare the source text with the standard patterns described here to identify which one it uses and how effectively it uses it. **Do not** use this file to write a new work during analysis.

From a reader’s perspective, the patterns here are industrial conventions for popular short fiction such as Fanqie short stories, Story Club tales, wife-chasing redemption, social-condition stories, rebirth, and revenge against abusers. They serve as the comparative source for “reusable structure / reversal design / genre formulas” and similar sections of the deconstruction report.

## Three-Layer Tag Contrast Method

### Three-Layer Structure

| Layer | Name | Meaning | Example |
|------|------|------|------|
| First | Identity tag | Identity visible to outsiders | Abandoned wife from a wealthy family |
| Second | Presentation tag | Behavior shown to outsiders | Endures silently and submits without resistance |
| Third | Core tag | True inner self | Calm, methodical, and advancing one calculated step at a time |

### Instructions

1. Fill all three tags for the character and create contrast among them—contrast creates dimensionality.
2. Identity and presentation tags may resemble each other to reinforce the stereotype, but the core tag must reverse it.
3. The larger the contrast, the more powerful the character’s “reveal moment.”
4. Every important character should contain contrast in at least one layer.
5. Express contrast through behavior rather than direct description:

```
身份标签：豪门弃妇
表现行为：被骂不还口，被赶不反抗
内核行为：悄悄录音、收集证据、提前转移财产
```

## Advanced Three-Layer Behavioral Contrast

After a relationship reaches the third layer—intimacy—the character can no longer perform behaviors that came easily during the first two layers.

| Relationship stage | Available behavior | Unavailable behavior |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| Identity-tag stage (strangers) | Flirt, act cold, maintain distance | — |
| Presentation-tag stage (familiar) | Continue the previous behavior pattern | — |
| Core-tag stage (intimate) | Reveal the true self | Earlier flirting or coldness becomes impossible |

### Instructions

1. Identify the character’s current relationship stage.
2. Behavioral contrast is itself part of characterization and must enter the plot.
3. Use “behavioral regression = deeper feeling” to create tension: the bold woman who flirted with the protagonist during the identity-tag stage becomes afraid to confess after intimacy develops.
4. Do not let a character maintain early-stage behavior after intimacy; doing so nullifies the contrast.

## Characterization Associations: Strong, Medium, and Weak

| Level | Definition | Function | Quantity |
|------|------|------|---------|
| Strong | A setting that directly affects plot direction or the core trope’s triumphant payoff | Drives character collisions and plot progression | At least three per character |
| Medium | A supporting character’s strong association, which must not overshadow the protagonist | Gives supporting characters function and recognizability | As needed |
| Weak | A personal-preference detail that enriches the character | Adds realism and memorability | Unlimited |

### Instructions

1. Classify the protagonist’s strength, wealth, connections, background, and other plot-shaping attributes as strong associations.
2. Confirm that every character has at least three strong associations.
3. Classify details such as “likes cake” and “enjoys looking at beautiful women” as weak associations used only for enrichment.
4. Keep weak associations from taking over; strong associations are the core that extends the plot.

## Characterization Rules

Characterization ≠ a character information sheet.

- Character information sheet = a collection of height, appearance, family background, and other external information.
- Characterization = functional design organized around the character’s “soul/distinctive personality.”
- The mission of characterization is to serve dramatic tension and emotional friction by letting personality drive behavior instead of forcing the character along the plot.

**Check**: if the design contains only height, weight, and interests without a functional “soul/distinctive personality,” redo it.

## Functional Supporting-Character Design

### Core Principles

1. A supporting character’s abilities and traits must closely connect to the plot.
2. Supporting characters exist to reveal the protagonist: their abilities serve the protagonist, and the protagonist helps them escape their predicament.

### Composite Supporting Character: The White Glove

| Function | Description |
|------|------|
| Speaks for the protagonist | Says what the protagonist should not say |
| Acts ruthlessly for the protagonist | Does what the protagonist should not do ruthlessly |
| Absolute defense | Firmly retaliates against anyone who infringes the protagonist’s interests |
| Rough exterior, careful mind | Records an antagonist’s conspiracy and plays it publicly to win support |
| Timely comedy | Continually supplies positive feedback and releases tension |

**Design principle**: keep the halo slightly weaker than the protagonist’s so it does not steal attention; give the character a striking entrance; have them immediately rebut doubts about the protagonist.

### Dimensional vs. Flat Characters

1. Not every character needs dimensionality; determine function first.
2. A flat character can remain mysterious and therefore more frightening or powerful.
3. “Redemption weakens by three; corruption strengthens threefold”—a master feels weaker after being fully humanized.
4. Design the tool the story needs; do not force dimensionality onto every character.

### Six Dimensions of Character Contrast

| Contrast | Operation |
|--------|------|
| Surface personality vs. inner personality | Smiling tiger/a sharp tongue with a soft heart |
| External appearance vs. surface personality | Looks fierce but is actually gentle |
| External appearance vs. inner personality | Looks gentle but is actually ruthless |
| Identity vs. lifestyle | Wealthy heir living in a basement |
| Identity vs. interests or expertise | Assassin skilled at flower arranging |
| Identity vs. inner personality | Soldier with an artistic inner life |

### Instructions

1. Before designing a supporting character, define the function: speaking for the protagonist, acting ruthlessly, providing comedy, or supplying information.
2. Select one or two contrast dimensions and use them to tag the character.
3. Control the supporting character’s halo so it never exceeds the protagonist’s.
4. Do not force functional supporting characters into dimensionality.

## Supporting-Character Logic

1. “Reverse the apparent role”: a seemingly positive action conceals antagonistic motivation.
2. Give supporting characters emotional armor: what appears cold and unfeeling is actually driven by deep emotion.
3. Prevent characterization collapse: behavioral choices must follow established character logic.
4. Begin with the genre’s established “tag characters”; mature-genre archetypes have already survived market testing.

## Methods for Emphasizing Characterization

### Instructions

1. Capture the highlight: identify the character’s most prominent trait and focus attention on demonstrating it.
2. Show characterization through action rather than narration—for example, after leaving prison, remain neither humble nor overbearing, fight for interests, and demonstrate worldly skill.
3. Use clashing contrasts to create tension.
4. Contrast-driven tension filters readers; control the degree because excessive contrast drives some away.

## Writing High-EQ or Intelligent-Character Scenes

### Instructions

1. Demonstrate emotional intelligence through concrete behavior within a scene, not narrator praise.
2. Write specific situations involving reading the room, improvising, controlling the scene, and taking over a conversational turn.
3. Base the performance on successful patterns in classic web fiction. Every scene must include at least one reversal in which readers expect embarrassment but the protagonist resolves it.
4. Every scene needs at least one “reversal”: readers anticipate awkwardness, but the protagonist resolves it perfectly.

## Use a Supporting Character as an Expectation Hub

### Instructions

1. Select one supporting character as a “mission base” who carries several short- and long-term expectations at once.
2. After the protagonist resolves an event and demonstrates superiority, have them return to this person to begin the next cycle.
3. Use the same person to launch new plot after every story unit.
4. When the person exits, create a larger benefit. Use “an accidental gain produces more” to transform the reader’s loss aversion.

## Ensemble Writing Method

### Instructions

1. Every character entrance must serve a function without filler; do not introduce a character whose function is unclear.
2. Relationships should have layers: guide, opponent, companion, obstacle.
3. Give different characters distinct speech styles and behavioral traits.
4. Show traits through behavior, not narration.

### Stereotyped Supporting-Character Description

1. Select one or two prominent traits as vivid tags: catchphrase, signature action, or extreme personality.
2. Tags make a character instantly memorable without consuming much space.
3. Functional supporting characters need a clear purpose: advance the plot, emphasize the protagonist, or provide information.

### Rhythm of Character Use

1. Limit a popular character’s appearance to once every three to five chapters, preserving anticipation without fatigue. Every appearance must add new information or a new function.
2. Let characters who fail to interest readers naturally leave the story.
3. Introduce new characters “through an existing character,” extending affection.

### Character-Pool Weaving

1. Let one character lead to others: if readers like the heroine → write her parents/sister/best friend → extend that affection to each newcomer.
2. Keep weaving like a web and quickly build an inexhaustible character pool.
3. Give every newcomer one vivid tag.

## Deepening Through Characterization

### Rules

First decide whether the current project benefits more from “story deepening” or “characterization deepening”:

| Condition | Priority | Action |
|------|------|------|
| Main line is already complex, with many factions and reversals | Deepen characterization | Keep story structure simple and create novelty through desire, contrast, and relational interaction |
| Main line is too direct; characters are vivid but events are insufficient | Deepen story | Add obstacles, changing goals, information gaps, or costs without adding functionless characters |
| Short or lightweight work | Deepen characterization | Let one or two strong characterization tags drive conflict without building a large world |
| Key or climactic long-form volume | Combine both | Raise the story by one dimension while visibly changing key relationships |

### Steps

1. Begin with a simple or even familiar story structure.
2. Add “dramatic qualities” to the characters—settings that target readers find entertaining.
3. Generate continuing plot through cycles of interaction among several characters.

### Cyclic Method: One Simple Plot Can Sustain Five or More Chapters

- Chapter 1: protagonist demonstrates superiority → martial uncle becomes more urgent.
- Chapter 2: martial uncle pressures a woman → her lover helps threaten and entice her.
- Chapter 3: woman approaches protagonist → lover secretly delights in it.
- Chapter 4: woman returns crying → lover pretends to comfort her while secretly delighted.
- Chapter 5: they report back for the reward → woman realizes the lover is not as pleased about the reward as expected.

## Character-Development Techniques

### Female Characters in Male-Oriented Fiction

1. Readers enjoy the heroine’s tags—noble and elegant, fair-skinned with long legs—not passages describing appearance.
2. Use stereotypes: vampire = noble elegance + exceptional beauty, so readers understand without explicit description.
3. Beauty is the cheapest resource in web fiction; do not spend extensive prose on it.
4. Personality can be intensified: extreme tsundere behavior, profound love for the protagonist, disregard for self-interest.
5. Preserve consistency after establishing the personality.

### The Protagonist May Err, Within Limits

| Forgivable | Unforgivable |
|--------|---------|
| Error caused by force majeure or an information gap | Error caused by stupidity or excessive misplaced mercy |
| — | Error incompatible with the character’s power, such as a supreme fighter acting foolishly because of an insignificant person |

### Inner–Outer Contrast in the Protagonist

1. Create contrast between surface and inner self—for example, outwardly lecherous but inwardly isolated.
2. Contrast adds depth and interest and creates rich possibilities for plot conflict.

### The Essence of an Antagonist

An antagonist is someone whose position and interests oppose the protagonist’s. Give antagonists their own thoughts and pursuits; do not write pure evil.

### Writing a Scoundrel Persona

1. Communicate the protagonist’s persona clearly at the opening and keep it consistent before and after transmigration.
2. Avoid hypocrisy: if the protagonist was not established as an old lecher, readers will resent a sudden pursuit of women later.
3. Explicit characterization = filter for readers who accept this person = expectation management.

## Character Memory-Anchor Method

1. Every character has at least one unusually prominent personality trait.
2. Only an exceptionally prominent feature becomes a memory anchor.
3. Goal: even without plot advancement, readers remain absorbed when several characters sit and talk.

---

## Bind Characterization to the Special Advantage

### Instructions

1. A special advantage does more than give the protagonist a superpower; its purpose is to make the protagonist’s personality reasonable.
2. Let the advantage’s rules constrain behavior → supporting-character reactions naturally shape the image → no direct description of the protagonist’s virtue is needed.
3. Pursue moral catharsis, the highest form of payoff: make the protagonist a “living saint” whose conduct earns readers’ wholehearted respect.

---

## Trope-Centered Characterization

### Instructions

1. Single labels such as “decisive killer” and “hot-blooded” are too vague → build the character through concrete recurring tropes → create a strong reader memory.
2. Example: every time the protagonist succeeds, a supporting character becomes envious and resentful → “You really deserve to die” → strong memory anchor plus humor.
3. Strong characterization + ordinary advantage + familiar formula can still produce unexpected appeal.

## Nine-Dimensional Characterization Framework

| Dimension | Content | Essential |
|------|------|------|
| Basic information | Previous life/current life and appearance | Build long-term expectation from the character |
| Personality | Contrasting characterization | Prepares the growth arc |
| Interests and habits | Greed/lust/praying for victims after killing | Repeatedly emphasize as a strong memory anchor |
| Relationships | More complex networks create more conflict | Every involved important figure has power and background |
| Background | Family/origin/upbringing | — |
| Growth arc | See the three stages below | — |
| Comparative contrast | Villain vs. good person/doctor saves lives → picks up a gun and kills | — |
| Values and attitudes | Different identities and statuses produce different views | Attitude = action objective |
| Character biography | Representative important events | A condensed life history |

### Three-Stage Character Growth Arc

| Stage | Core | Corresponds to |
|------|------|------|
| Small self (childhood) | Constructs a self and wants to become who others see | Motivation |
| Individual self (youth) | Encounters the real self and suffers between the two | Attitude |
| Social self (maturity) | Makes the self seen by others into the pursued goal | Values |

Emotional formula: satisfaction (identification) → blow (sympathy) → doubt (resonance) → heartbreak (empathy).

## Four Relationship-Stage Tags

| Stage | Degree of knowledge | Presentation |
|------|---------|------|
| Meeting (strangers) | Knows only appearance | Vigilant/keeps distance/guards |
| Acquaintance (passersby) | Preliminary understanding without knowing limits | Reads the room/flattering |
| Mutual understanding (close friends) | Knows disposition and limits | Risqué banter/reveals inner thoughts/shares troubles |
| Romance (lovers) | Understands inner wounds | Possessive/talkative/works tirelessly without complaint |

## Understand Web-Fiction Relationships Through Workplace Thinking

### Rules

The essence of web fiction: achieve complete catharsis in one “workplace,” then move to another and do it again.

### Commonalities and Differences

| Shared dimension | Description |
|---------|------|
| Hierarchy | Every environment contains superior–subordinate relationships |
| Mutual assistance | Cooperation and mutual benefit among peers |
| Mutual harm | Competition and infighting among peers |

| Differing dimension | Description |
|---------|------|
| Mode of cooperation | Different environments use different patterns |
| Resource type | Different environments contest different resources |
| Individual ability | The protagonist’s unique advantage |

### Steps

1. Design a “workplace environment” for the protagonist: school/sect/company/military.
2. Establish hierarchy: superior/peer/subordinate/opponent.
3. Use hierarchy to generate contradiction and conflict naturally.
4. After the protagonist achieves complete catharsis in this environment, move to a larger “workplace.”

## Identification-Building Method

### Rules

- Maintain identification by keeping pacing and the emotional chain unbroken. A break occurs when readers begin judging the plot by real-world logic.
- Once readers begin applying real-world logic, pacing has failed.
- The core requirement is an unbroken emotional chain.

### Methods

| Method | Practice | Effect |
|------|------|------|
| Empathetic entry point | Use a common everyday event | Instantly reduces distance |
| Authentic detail | Fill with details of ordinary life | Strengthens the world’s reality |
| Save-the-cat event | Use one opening event to emphasize an aspect of characterization | Readers quickly understand and identify with the protagonist |
| Prerequisite for empathy | Reveal the character’s authentic side, weakness, or predicament | Creates a protective impulse in readers |
| Relatable anxiety | Use a universally understood predicament such as “lacking money” | Produces natural empathy |

### Three Levels of Empathy

1. **Surface empathy**: similar experience.
2. **Deep empathy**: similar emotion.
3. **Identification**: aligned values.

### Behaviors That Break Identification (Avoid)

- A character makes a choice readers cannot understand.
- Large blocks of setting exposition interrupt the narrative.
- Slow pacing breaks the emotional chain.
- The protagonist’s values seriously diverge from the target reader’s.

---

## Managing Safety and Core Emotion

1. Readers’ need for safety varies by genre; identify the genre first.
2. In high-tension genres such as extreme-dao stories, maximizing safety erases danger and shifts the core emotion. Do not give the protagonist an excessively strong patron.
3. Never give the protagonist a powerful patron who can resolve a crisis at any time. A patron’s presence must not weaken urgency. Mark violations as “excessive patron.”
4. When capacity is limited, plot always comes first; worldbuilding is an enhancement.

## Quality Checklist

After completing character design, check each item:

- [ ] Does the character have three layers of tags—identity/presentation/core—with contrast in at least one?
- [ ] Are there at least three strong associations?
- [ ] Is the characterization functional design or merely accumulated appearance data?
- [ ] Does every supporting character have a clear function without overshadowing the protagonist?
- [ ] Does every character have at least one memory anchor?
- [ ] Does every supporting-character appearance serve a function without filler?
- [ ] Do different characters have distinct speech styles and behavioral traits?
- [ ] Are the protagonist’s errors within forgivable limits?
- [ ] Does the antagonist have independent logic and pursuits rather than pure evil?
- [ ] Do characterization and conduct remain consistent without collapse?
- [ ] Is the emotional chain of identification continuous and uninterrupted by setting exposition?
- [ ] Does safety match the genre’s needs, without an overpowered patron in a tension-driven genre?
- [ ] Is high emotional intelligence demonstrated through concrete plot rather than narrator praise?
