# Character Design Operations Manual

> Purpose: an instruction set for character-design methods. Use the decision guide to choose a method, then execute its steps.

## Three-Layer Label Contrast

### Structure

| Layer | Name | Meaning | Example |
|------|------|------|------|
| First | Identity label | Identity the outside world sees | Abandoned wife from a wealthy family |
| Second | Presentation label | Behavior shown to outsiders | Endures in silence and submits |
| Third | Core label | Genuine inner nature | Calm, methodical, always planning ahead |

### Instructions

1. Fill all three layers and create contrast among them. Contrast creates dimensionality.
2. Identity and presentation may align to reinforce a stereotype, but the core must reverse it.
3. Greater contrast creates a more powerful “reveal your hand” moment.
4. Every important character should contain at least one contrasting layer.
5. Express contrast through behavior, not direct description:

```
身份标签：豪门弃妇
表现行为：被骂不还口，被赶不反抗
内核行为：悄悄录音、收集证据、提前转移财产
```

## Advanced Three-Layer Behavioral Contrast

After a relationship reaches the third layer—intimacy—the character may become unable to perform behaviors that were easy during the first two stages.

| Relationship stage | Available behavior | Unavailable behavior |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| Identity-label stage (strangers) | Teasing, coldness, maintaining distance | — |
| Presentation-label stage (familiar) | Continue the earlier behavioral pattern | — |
| Core-label stage (intimate) | Reveal the true self | Earlier teasing or coldness becomes difficult |

### Instructions

1. Identify the current relationship stage.
2. Behavioral contrast is part of characterization and must enter the plot.
3. Use “behavioral regression = emotional deepening” to create tension. A bold woman who teased the protagonist as a stranger may become unable to confess after they grow close.
4. Do not preserve all early-stage behavior during intimacy, or the contrast disappears.

## Characterization Connections: Strong, Medium, and Weak

| Level | Definition | Function | Quantity |
|------|------|------|---------|
| Strong | Settings that directly affect plot direction, the core hook, and major payoffs | Drive collisions and plot progress | At least three per character |
| Medium | Strong connections for supporting characters that do not eclipse the protagonist | Give supporting characters function and identity | As needed |
| Weak | Personal preferences that add texture | Increase realism and memorability | Unlimited |

### Instructions

1. Classify attributes that affect plot direction—power, wealth, connections, background—as strong connections.
2. Confirm at least three strong connections per character.
3. Classify details such as “likes cake” or “enjoys looking at beautiful people” as weak connections used only for texture.
4. Do not let weak connections dominate. Strong connections are the engine for extending plot.

## Characterization Rules

Characterization ≠ character data sheet.

- Character data sheet = accumulated external facts such as height, appearance, and family background.
- Characterization = functional design built around a character's “soul” or distinctive personality.
- Its mission is to serve dramatic tension and emotional push-pull by letting personality drive behavior instead of plot forcing behavior.

**Check**: If the design contains only height, weight, and hobbies without a functional “soul” or distinctive personality, redo it.

## Functional Supporting-Character Design

### Core Principles

1. A supporting character's abilities and traits must connect tightly to the plot.
2. Supporting characters exist to reveal the protagonist. Their abilities support the protagonist, and the protagonist helps them escape their predicaments.

### Multifunctional Supporting Character: The “White Glove”

| Function | Description |
|------|------|
| Speaks for the protagonist | Says what the protagonist should not say |
| Acts ruthlessly for the protagonist | Makes threats the protagonist should not make |
| Absolute defense | Strikes back decisively at anyone who harms the protagonist's interests |
| Coarse but careful | Records an antagonist's conspiracy and plays it back to win public support |
| Timely comedy | Delivers positive feedback and relieves tension |

**Design principle**: Keep their aura slightly weaker than the protagonist's. Give them a striking entrance, and make them rebut doubts about the protagonist immediately.

### Round vs. Flat Characters

1. Not every character needs dimension; evaluate function first.
2. A flat character can remain mysterious and therefore more frightening or powerful.
3. “Redemption weakens by three; corruption strengthens threefold.” Humanizing a supreme expert can reduce their power.
4. Design the tool the story needs. Do not force every character to become rounded.

### Six Dimensions of Character Contrast

| Contrast | Operation |
|--------|------|
| Surface personality vs. inner personality | Smiling tiger / sharp tongue with a soft heart |
| Appearance vs. surface personality | Looks fierce, acts gentle |
| Appearance vs. inner personality | Looks gentle, is ruthless |
| Identity vs. lifestyle | Wealthy heir living in a basement |
| Identity vs. hobby or skill | Assassin skilled at flower arranging |
| Identity vs. inner personality | Soldier with a poetic soul |

### Instructions

1. First define the supporting character's function: speak for the protagonist, act ruthlessly, provide comedy, or supply information.
2. Choose one or two dimensions of contrast and label the character.
3. Keep the supporting character's aura below the protagonist's.
4. Do not force a purely functional character into unnecessary complexity.

## Logic for Supporting Characters

1. “Write the positive scene in reverse”: a seemingly positive action conceals antagonistic motivation.
2. Emotional armor: apparent coldness conceals a deeper emotional drive.
3. Avoid characterization collapse; every choice must follow the established logic.
4. Begin with proven “genre-label characters.” Archetypes in mature genres have already been market-tested.

## Highlighting Characterization

### Instructions

1. Find the standout trait and focus attention on it.
2. Reveal it through action, not narration—for example, after leaving prison the character remains composed, protects their interests, and shows experience.
3. Use colliding contrasts to create tension.
4. Contrast filters readers, so control the scale; extreme contrast can drive part of the audience away.

---

## Writing Highly Perceptive or Intelligent Characters

### Instructions

1. Demonstrate emotional intelligence in concrete scenes; do not praise it in narration.
2. Write specific situations involving reading the room, improvising, taking control, and keeping conversation moving.
3. Draw on successful patterns from classic web fiction. Every beat needs at least one reversal in which readers expect embarrassment but the protagonist resolves it.
4. Every beat must contain at least one such reversal.

## Supporting Character as an Anticipation Hub

### Instructions

1. Choose one supporting character as a “mission base” who carries several short- and long-term expectations.
2. After each victory or display, the protagonist returns to that person to begin the next round.
3. Use the same person to launch new plot after every story unit.
4. When the character leaves the story, attach a larger benefit. An accidental greater gain converts readers' loss aversion.

## Ensemble Writing

### Instructions

1. Every character entrance needs a function; do not introduce filler.
2. Relationships need layers: guide, opponent, companion, obstacle.
3. Give different characters distinct speaking styles and behavioral traits.
4. Reveal traits through behavior, not narration.

### Archetypal Supporting Characters

1. Choose one or two prominent traits as vivid labels: catchphrase, signature action, or extreme temperament.
2. Labels make characters instantly memorable without consuming much space.
3. Functional characters need an explicit job: advance plot, elevate the protagonist, or provide information.

### Rhythm of Character Use

1. Let a popular character appear once every three to five chapters to preserve anticipation without causing fatigue. Every appearance must add information or function.
2. Retire characters readers do not care about naturally.
3. Introduce new characters through beloved existing ones so affection carries over.

### Weaving a Character Pool

1. Let one character lead to another: if readers like the heroine, introduce her parents, sister, or best friend and carry that affection forward.
2. Keep weaving the network to create a renewable character pool.
3. Give every new character one clear label.

## Deepening Through Characterization

### Rule

First decide whether the project needs deeper story or deeper characterization:

| Condition | Priority | Action |
|------|------|------|
| Main plot is already complex, with many factions and reversals | Characterization | Keep story structure simple; create freshness through desires, contrasts, and relationships |
| Main plot is too straight; characters are vivid but events are sparse | Story | Add obstacles, changing goals, information gaps, or costs without adding functionless characters |
| Short or lightweight work | Characterization | Let one or two strong labels drive conflict; avoid extensive worldbuilding |
| Key or climactic long-form volume | Both | Escalate story by one dimension and visibly change a key relationship |

### Steps

1. Start with a simple or even familiar story structure.
2. Add “dramatic potential” to the characters: settings the target readers will enjoy.
3. Sustain plot through repeating interactions among a small cast.

### Loop Example: One Simple Event Across Five or More Chapters

- Chapter 1: protagonist shows off → martial uncle becomes more urgent
- Chapter 2: martial uncle pressures a woman → her lover helps threaten and entice her
- Chapter 3: woman approaches protagonist → lover secretly delights
- Chapter 4: woman returns in tears → lover pretends to comfort her while privately pleased
- Chapter 5: report and collect reward → woman notices her lover is less pleased than expected

## Characterization Techniques

### Female Characters in Male-Audience Fiction

1. Readers like the heroine's labels—noble elegance, pale skin, long legs—not extended physical description.
2. Use stereotypes efficiently: “vampire” already implies elegance and striking beauty.
3. Beauty is the cheapest resource in web fiction; do not spend large amounts of prose on it.
4. Personality may be extreme: exceptionally tsundere, deeply devoted to the protagonist, unconcerned with personal benefit.
5. Preserve consistency after establishing the trait.

### Protagonists May Err, Within Limits

| Forgivable | Unforgivable |
|--------|---------|
| Mistakes caused by force majeure or information gaps | Mistakes caused by stupidity or excessive sanctimony |
| — | Errors inconsistent with established power, such as an emperor-tier expert acting foolishly because of a minor figure |

### External/Internal Protagonist Contrast

1. Contrast surface and inner self, such as a publicly lecherous persona hiding an isolated inner life.
2. Contrast adds depth and playfulness while creating more plot-conflict possibilities.

### Essence of an Antagonist

An antagonist is someone whose position and interests oppose the protagonist's. Give them ideas and pursuits; do not write pure evil.

### Writing a Deliberately “Trashy” Persona

1. Communicate the protagonist's nature clearly at the opening and keep it consistent before and after transmigration.
2. Avoid hypocrisy. If the protagonist was never established as lustful, a sudden pursuit of women repels readers.
3. Clear characterization filters for accepting readers and manages expectations.

## Character Memory-Point Method

1. Every character needs at least one unusually prominent personality trait.
2. A trait becomes memorable only when emphasized strongly.
3. Goal: even when plot does not advance, readers enjoy watching the cast sit together and talk.

## Bind Characterization to the Cheat Ability

### Instructions

1. A cheat ability should do more than grant supernatural power; it should rationalize the protagonist's personality.
2. Let its rules constrain behavior → supporting-character reactions naturally build the image → no direct praise of the protagonist's virtue is needed.
3. Pursue moral satisfaction, the highest form of payoff: make the protagonist a “living saint” whose actions genuinely earn reader respect.

---

## Trope-Centered Characterization

### Instructions

1. Single labels such as “decisive killer” or “hot-blooded” are too vague. Build the character through concrete recurring bits that create strong recall.
2. Example: whenever the protagonist succeeds, a supporting character cycles through envy and resentment, saying, “You really deserve to die.” This creates humor and a memory point.
3. Strong characterization + an ordinary cheat ability + familiar tropes can still create unexpected appeal.

## Nine-Dimension Character Framework

| Dimension | Content | Key point |
|------|------|------|
| Basic information | Prior/current life and appearance | Start with character to establish long-range anticipation |
| Personality | Contrasting characterization | Prepare the growth arc |
| Hobbies/habits | Greed, lust, praying over victims after killing | Repeat as a strong memory point |
| Relationships | More complex networks generate more conflict | Every connected important figure has power and background |
| Background | Family/origin/formative environment | — |
| Growth arc | See the three stages below | — |
| Contrast | Villain vs. good person; doctor saves lives → takes up a gun to kill | — |
| Values and attitudes | Different identities and positions produce different views | Attitude = action goal |
| Character biography | Representative important stories | A compressed life history |

### Three-Stage Growth Arc

| Stage | Core | Corresponds to |
|------|------|------|
| Small self (childhood) | Invented self: wants to become the person others see | Motivation |
| Individual self (youth) | Real self: suffers between the two | Attitude |
| Social self (maturity) | The self others see becomes the pursued goal | Values |

Emotional formula: satisfaction (recognition) → blow (sympathy) → doubt (resonance) → heartbreak (empathy)

## Four Relationship-Stage Labels

| Stage | Understanding | Behavior |
|------|---------|------|
| Meeting (strangers) | Appearance only | Vigilance/distance/defenses |
| Acquaintance (passing connection) | Preliminary understanding, unknown limits | Reading the room/flattery |
| Mutual understanding (close friends) | Knows temperament and limits | Dirty jokes/opening up/sharing troubles |
| Romance (lovers) | Knows inner scars | Possessiveness/talking constantly/tireless devotion |

## Understanding Web-Fiction Relationships Through Workplace Structure

### Rule

Web fiction often lets the protagonist triumph completely in one “workplace,” then move to another.

### Commonalities and Differences

| Common dimension | Description |
|---------|------|
| Hierarchy | Every environment has superiors and subordinates |
| Mutual aid | Cooperation and mutual benefit among peers |
| Mutual harm | Competition and infighting among peers |

| Difference | Description |
|---------|------|
| Cooperation | Different environments use different models |
| Resources | Different environments contest different assets |
| Individual ability | The protagonist's unique advantage |

### Steps

1. Design a “workplace environment” for the protagonist: school, sect, company, or army.
2. Establish ranks: superior, peer, subordinate, opponent.
3. Let hierarchy naturally generate contradiction and conflict.
4. After the protagonist triumphs there, move to a larger “workplace.”

## Building Reader Identification

### Rules

- Preserve identification by keeping pace and the emotional chain unbroken. The chain has broken when readers begin judging the plot by real-world logic.
- Once readers switch to real-world logic, pacing has failed.
- The emotional chain is the core.

### Methods

| Method | Action | Effect |
|------|------|------|
| Empathy entry point | Use a common event from daily life | Immediately closes distance |
| Authentic detail | Add lived-in details | Strengthens the world's reality |
| Save-the-cat beat | Use an opening event to stress one aspect of the protagonist | Readers quickly understand and identify |
| Basis for empathy | Expose a genuine weakness or predicament | Creates a protective response |
| Relatable anxiety | Use a universally understood problem such as lack of money | Creates natural empathy |

### Three Levels of Empathy

1. **Surface empathy**: similar experience
2. **Deep empathy**: similar emotion
3. **Identification**: shared values

### Behaviors That Break Identification

- A choice readers cannot understand
- A long setting explanation that interrupts narrative
- Dragging pace that breaks the emotional chain
- Protagonist values far removed from target readers

---

## Safety and Core Emotional Control

1. Different genres require different degrees of security; identify the genre first.
2. In tension-driven genres such as extreme martial-arts fiction, overwhelming safety eliminates danger and shifts the core emotion. Do not give the protagonist an excessively powerful patron.
3. Never create a patron who can resolve any crisis at any time. Their existence must not reduce urgency. Mark violations as “overpowered patron.”
4. When resources are limited, plot always comes first; worldbuilding is an enhancement.

---

## Quality Checklist

After character design, check each item:

- [ ] Three labels—identity, presentation, core—with at least one contrast
- [ ] At least three strong connections
- [ ] Functional characterization rather than accumulated appearance facts
- [ ] Supporting characters have clear functions and do not eclipse the protagonist
- [ ] Every character has at least one memory point
- [ ] Every supporting-character entrance serves a function without filler
- [ ] Different characters speak and behave differently
- [ ] Protagonist mistakes remain forgivable
- [ ] Antagonists have personal logic and pursuits rather than pure evil
- [ ] Words and actions remain consistent without characterization collapse
- [ ] The emotional chain of identification remains continuous and is not interrupted by exposition
- [ ] Security level matches the genre; tense genres do not give the protagonist an overpowering patron
- [ ] High emotional intelligence appears through concrete plot, not narratorial praise
