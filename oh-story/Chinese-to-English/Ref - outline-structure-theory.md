## Level-One and Level-Two Structures

### Level-One Structure = Linked Episodes

A story line consists of several independent episodes, with the protagonist acting as the thread that connects them all.

- Advantage: Dense payoffs and a simple structure that is difficult to mishandle
- Disadvantage: Cannot support great length, offers only one form of anticipation, and has a limited ceiling

### Level-Two Structure = Nested, Expanded Episodes

Stretch each “episode” from a level-one structure across an entire volume, making every volume a complete story. The central problem is that unfolding only one story across 200,000–300,000 Chinese characters creates excessive setup.

**Solution = nesting**: Each structural part of the larger story is itself a smaller story. The smaller stories deliver payoffs while quietly setting up the larger story. They advance layer by layer, then connect and detonate at the climax.

### Level-Three Structure

Volumes are no longer isolated. They form one unified story structure in which every plot event connects to and affects the others. This demands exceptional skill in weaving plot.

### Special Principles for Long-Form Fiction

- Smallest unit of conflict = beat (one action matched with one reaction)
- Transition chapters often contain too much information setup and too little conflict
- Two parts of screenwriting theory do not apply: ① character arcs—the protagonist should have matured long before a story reaches several million Chinese characters, so the protagonist may function as a tool; ② meticulous detail—long-form readers do not closely study subtext

## Basic Story Structure (Protagonist -> Obstacle -> Goal)

**Core model**: protagonist -> obstacle -> goal

Three improvements from basic to strong:
1. **Add complications**: A story needs multiple obstacles
2. **Increase difficulty**: Equal difficulty is monotonous and decreasing difficulty is dull; it must keep rising
3. **Add the unexpected**: Just as the protagonist is about to reach the goal, introduce a surprise—the plan fails. Solutions: a flash of inspiration (an idea conceived on the spot) or a mysterious smile (a long-hidden move)

### Two-Way Connection Between Subplot and Main Plot

Tunnel from the subplot side and the main-plot side simultaneously until they meet, ensuring the earlier and later material connects. Keep the subplot from becoming an “isolated case”: a broad policy filters down through every level, and the protagonist’s experience is only one cross-section of the larger phenomenon.

### Connecting Short Stories into a Long Story

A long story is a chain of short stories, and hooks are the links. Before a short story ends, something must remain unresolved. A hook also provides direction.

## Three-Act and Five-Act Structures

### The Essence of Story: Fact -> Event -> Story

- **Fact**: A static presentation, not a story
- **Event**: A changing fact—a movement from X to Y
- **Story**: The product of selecting, ordering, and causally connecting events
- **Causality creates meaning**: Once causal logic is introduced, readers ask “why?” and meaning arises naturally

### Techniques for Building Causal Chains

| Technique | Explanation |
|------|------|
| Ordering | Rearrange chronological order to establish causality |
| Setup | Before Event B, plant its cause beside Event A |
| Foreshadowing | Event A + the cause of Event H, revealed only after many intervening events |
| Hook | Plant the cause of Event E beside Event D to create suspense |

### Five Acts: The Optimal Template for Bending a Causal Chain

| Act | Rule | Causal-Chain State |
|----|------|-----------|
| Act One: Opening | The cause must be planted here | Seed |
| Act Two: Development | Effect + cause, developing into the next act’s cause | Growth |
| Act Three: Turn | Effect + cause, qualitatively changing into the next act’s cause | Turn |
| Act Four: Action | Effect + cause, reaching white heat | Sprint |
| Act Five: Ending | Effect, resolving the chain | Completion |

**Five-act rule**: The causal chain must have five links; each link has a fixed function; do not skip steps or change the order.

### Relationship Among Three Acts, Four-Part Structure, and Five Acts

- **Three acts** (conflict begins -> opposition -> resolution): Use when the world’s conflict is strong enough to exist without the protagonist driving it, such as an apocalypse or natural disaster
- **Four acts / setup, development, turn, conclusion**: Use when outside forces push the protagonist forward too quickly to resist, leaving them to suffer passively, such as the early stage of a regretful-partner reconciliation story or a fatalistic situation
- **Five acts**: Use when the protagonist is cornered and actively counterattacks, as in wish-fulfillment or revenge fiction

**The essence of a turn**: A qualitative change in the nature of the conflict—the conflict becomes more severe and the protagonist’s situation worsens. Quietly plant the cause of the turn in advance.

### Web Fiction’s Sixth Act: Aftermath (The Deeper Logic of Showing Off)

The **fundamental conflict** in showing off is “the protagonist versus society’s perception,” not the protagonist versus the villain.

The protagonist’s strength has two dimensions:
- **Visible track**: Advancement within the combat-power system
- **Hidden track**: Society’s perception and classification of the protagonist

When the visible track exceeds the hidden track, conflict emerges -> the protagonist shows off -> the hidden track rises until they match. A small imbalance creates a small display; a large imbalance creates a major display.

The correct way to show off: Have the protagonist rarely insist “I am strong.” Instead, let the environment, other people, and time tell society, “You truly have grown stronger.”

### Six Formulaic Modules for Standardized Web-Fiction Writing

1. **Set the tone** (the essence of the golden first three chapters): Fulfill reader expectations. Core formula: unsettling circumstances + status that urgently needs improvement + a hopeful external advantage + an appealing future readers can envision
2. **Special-advantage design**: Information advantage (best, least likely to lose control) > accelerated progression > item-summoning advantage (easy to lose control). Core: Keep it simple, with clear functions and advancement rules
3. **Archetypal characters**: Give each character a label so one trait immediately recalls the character
4. **Progression pacing**: goal -> action -> completion -> reward loop, with short cycles
5. **Dense payoff bombardment**: dissatisfaction -> removal of dissatisfaction = payoff
6. **Formulaic conflict**: The simpler the conflict itself, the easier it is to understand; the more complex the suspense, the more engaging it becomes

## Five-Level Progressive Outline Expansion

| Level | Explanation |
|------|------|
| Level 1 | Coordinates the entire text; summarizes the book in one sentence |
| Level 2 | One story plot = one level-two main plot |
| Level 3 | Concrete plot |
| Level 4 | More detailed plot extrapolation, such as conflict with a cousin -> humiliation -> determination -> special advantage -> kill the opponent |
| Level 5 | The most detailed process, such as exactly how the special advantage is acquired |

**Each lower level serves the level above it.** Level 3 is sufficient to support a long story; Level 5 easily becomes chaotic. Working from the top down prevents blocks; working from the bottom up easily causes blocks and drift from the main plot.

### Hidden-Track Design

- A hidden track is a line readers cannot see, creating surprise
- It coordinates the entire text, never stated explicitly from beginning to end but supported by foreshadowing
- **A clue spanning a thousand miles** = the answer is given at the opening, but readers recognize it only at the end
- Mysteries often run several hidden tracks simultaneously -> merge them -> merge them with other hidden tracks -> ultimately coordinate the entire text

### Planning Eight Tracks

Three or four tracks are enough—the main plot (advances the goal) + romance arc + one or two subplots or hidden tracks (plant foreshadowing), with every lower-level track serving the higher level.

## Choosing an Outline Structure: Plot-Driven vs. Character-Driven

### Strong Main Plot (Level-Two Structure)

Typical genres: progression fiction and mystery. Plot outweighs character; supporting characters serve as tools.

Core traits:
- Every volume has a complete three-act structure, and every act contains a smaller three-act structure (nesting)
- There is only one main plot, and every subplot advances it

**Major-hook design**: Divide the main plot into major subplots; each final objective becomes a major hook.
- Major hooks must be continuous; as soon as one ends, another begins
- Changing major hooks requires setup and cannot feel abrupt

### Weak Main Plot (Level-One Structure)

Typical genres: overpowered-protagonist and slice-of-life fiction. Character outweighs plot.

Core traits:
- Episodic format, each with a complete three-act structure
- Formula: core payoff + appealing characterization + episodic stage
- No explicit main plot is required; the protagonist links everything
- Fresh characterization is the focus

### Transitional Type

Begin with a weak main plot and episodic daily life, then gradually transition into a strong main plot.

### Subplot Design Principles

Every subplot must connect to the main plot, or it is wasted. A subplot character’s identity should naturally serve the main plot.

## Traditional-Fiction Framework and Four Retention Methods

### Framework for Each Volume (Purpose + Climactic Ending)

Framework = bone structure; plot = appearance. Accomplish two things in each volume: ① define the purpose of arriving in the new location ② set a major climactic ending.

### Four Major Retention Methods

1. **Progression**: Not only combat strength, but also status and money
2. **Resource dilemma**: Cultivation requires resources; seeking them is an effective way to retain readers
3. **Set goals**: Major goals + minor goals + false goals, which need not be achieved but create anticipation
4. **Solve mysteries**: Reveal the tip of the iceberg -> solve layer by layer -> continuously clear the fog

## Map Changes and Map Architecture

### Four Generations of Map-Change Approaches

| Generation | Core | Problem |
|------|------|------|
| 1.0 Ascension progression | Mortal realm -> immortal realm -> divine realm | Discards every relationship |
| 2.0 Lotus-map method | Central lotus-heart map + petal branches | Spatial limitations |
| 3.0 New approaches | Clan progression / simulator / version updates | Requires setup beginning in the middle stages |

### Three Map-Change Methods

1. **Thousand-thread method**: Continuously stitch connections between two major maps
2. **Keep the major map**: Change only smaller maps while remaining within one larger map
3. **Lotus-map method**: Unfold maps layer by layer like a lotus

### Top-Level Framework Method (Preventing Collapse)

Before starting the book, define all top-tier powers on the full map as “pillars.” Extend downward from the top: region -> small clan -> small sect -> techniques and treasures. Every character who appears must connect to one top-level power.

**Ruler mindset**: Define top-tier displays of strength in advance as benchmarks. Opening boss = 5, middle boss = 50, and only late-stage bosses reach 100.

### Relationships-First Method

Before moving to the next map, extend the relationship network into it. Core: Relationships move first -> the protagonist moves afterward.

### Key Principles of Map Changes

- A major map transition may lose one-third of returning readers
- Lost readers never return
- If later material is strong, the returning-reader count can rise again

## Multi-Track Extension Techniques

### Parallel Tracks and Opening and Closing Loops

Plant foreshadowing for subplots within the main plot from the opening. Develop the main plot and at least two subplots simultaneously; whenever the main plot pauses -> a subplot enters. Continuously open and close loops so the plot keeps extending.

### Multiple Tracks Reduce Fatigue

Insert half a chapter from a supporting character’s POV about an unrelated event in the middle of a long plot -> provide brief relief. Transition naturally: plant subplot foreshadowing in the main plot -> develop it appropriately -> return to the main plot.

## Western-Fantasy Essentials

**Subject matter**: Choose evergreen forms such as D&D-style fantasy, wizards, farming, or lord-management fiction. Avoid high-martial-arts and Japanese-style fantasy.

**Worldbuilding**: Reveal it through dialogue and behavior rather than long expository narration.

**Characters**: Use Chinese phonetic equivalents for translated names to lower the barrier to entry; first design the special advantage’s traits, then build the character’s personality around it. Moral satisfaction is the highest form of payoff.

## Level-One Structure = A Comparable Title’s Plot Unit (The Axis of Pacing Comparison)

A level-one structure is one complete episode: a conflict develops from its beginning through eruption and resolution, covering roughly 15,000–30,000 Chinese characters or several chapters. The “plot units / chapter summaries / payoff loops” extracted when deconstructing a comparable title form its sequence of level-one structures; the “plot units” in your own volume outline are also level-one structures. Thus, “level-one structure” is the theoretical term, while the actual artifact is a **plot unit** (on the comparable side, an extracted plot unit; on this book’s side, a plot-unit card in the volume outline). Pacing comparison is therefore not a separate process: preserve the emotion and function of the comparable title’s level-one structural sequence, replace the material, and transfer it into your volume outline.

### Key-Point Method (A Unified Rhythmic Anchor at Every Scale)

Every scale—the full book, volume, level-one structure, subplot, or chapter—has five key points: opening, quarter point, midpoint, three-quarter point, and ending. The quarter point, midpoint, and three-quarter point are positions for key plot events. There are three types:

| Type | Meaning |
|------|------|
| Plot inversion | The development is within reader expectations, but the protagonist’s solution is spectacular, such as showing off and humiliating an opponent or making a heroic rescue |
| Plot turn | An unexpected development, such as an identity reversal, an enemy becoming an ally, or a sudden change in circumstances |
| Inciting event | The first fate-changing moment in a book or volume that directly relates to the central main plot |

Not every scale needs all three positions filled, but each volume should place at least one key plot event at the quarter point, midpoint, and three-quarter point to prevent uniform intensity throughout.

### Transferring Pacing from a Comparable Title (Do Once Before Finalizing the Volume Outline; Skip if No Comparable Exists)

1. **Select a passage by plot unit**: In the primary comparable, identify the sequence of level-one structures to use for this volume. The unit of selection is the plot unit produced during deconstruction (`剧情/{剧情标题}.md`). Search in this order: First read the primary comparable’s “plot-unit list” in `剧情/README.md`. If a list exists, each book uses one file; locate the same type directly by “type / sequence tags.” If there is no list, search `剧情/*.md` using the “type” line (the first key, a required enum) + the “sequence tags / formula-framework position” line (the second key). If nothing matches, fall back to selecting by chapter range without blocking the process. With multiple comparables, use the outline budget in `cross-book-recall.md` to supplement matching entries from secondary comparables. You must read the authoritative file `剧情/节奏.md` for eruption density and payoff loops. If it is missing, repair or rerun deconstruction Stage 3 before continuing; do not guess pacing from summaries. Number of comparable level-one structures for this volume ≈ volume length ÷ 15,000–30,000 Chinese characters.
2. **Mark key points**: Identify which key plot events the comparable places at the quarter point, midpoint, and three-quarter point of those level-one structures, and classify each as an inversion, turn, or inciting event. Plot points whose summary has “type = turning point,” together with abrupt tonal changes such as warmth → oppression, are deterministic anchors for key points rather than matters of intuition. The plot unit’s “structural distribution” (setup / development / climax / resolution phase) and “plot-point index” are also deterministic anchors.
3. **Replace the material**: Preserve the comparable’s emotional chain and functional positions, while replacing every object, setting, and event with your own. Never copy a specific sequence. Take the functional-position list from the replaceable items in the authoritative file `剧情/情绪模块.md`. If it is missing, repair it first; do not generate a substitute list.
4. **Correct structural flaws**: Before replacing the material, consult “Not Recommended for Imitation” in `拆文报告.md`. If the comparable handles a key point weakly—known flaws such as slow-burn omission or leaving the protagonist on the sidelines too long—add the missing key plot event at that position or move a later key event forward instead of copying the weak handling. A key point must not lose its force merely because the comparable was careless.
5. **Arrange chapters + place them in the volume outline**: Put each replaced and corrected key plot event at the same relative position in this volume. Here, the quarter point, midpoint, and three-quarter point operate at the **volume scale** (quarter point ≈ total chapters × 0.25, midpoint × 0.5, three-quarter point × 0.75). When the volume contains several level-one structures, each also has its own setup → inversion → resolution; do not confuse those with volume-scale key points. Use ordinary advancement chapters between key points, and add one breathing or relationship chapter after a key point when needed. Record each event in the volume outline’s “comparable-structure coordinates” and the emotional arc’s chapter-position column; when creating the detailed outline, mark each chapter’s function. Also add the selected plot unit to the plot-unit card’s “comparable plot reference” in the form {book title} *Plot Title*.

Fine nearby, vague at a distance: Arrange only the current volume precisely. Rough coordinates are enough for later volumes; there is no need to plan the whole book at once.

A minimal example (the demo *让你管账号，你高燃混剪炸全网*): At the comparable structure’s quarter point, “an unknown underdog presents a rough work and nobody believes in it”; at the midpoint, “the work goes viral and attracts attention inside and outside the establishment”; at the three-quarter point, “professional forces step in to replace the creator, but instead prove that the protagonist cannot be replicated.” After transferring this to Jiang Chen’s military-publicity plot, the quarter point becomes his filming *诸君，且听龙吟* on a phone—the image quality is rough, but he captures the military spirit first; the midpoint becomes the video exploding across the internet and igniting an enlistment wave; the three-quarter point becomes a professional team reshooting it in high definition, only for Zhou Bosen to point out on the spot that it “lacks a soul,” after which Zhang Yaozu ultimately approves continued use of the original phone footage. What is preserved is the emotional chain “dismissal → market validation → authoritative replacement fails → value confirmed.” The material, setting, and character relationships all come from this book.

### Produce Detailed Outlines by Plot Batch (Detailed-Outline Batch Boundaries)

One detailed-outline batch = one plot unit in the volume outline, roughly 5–15 chapters. Convert scale this way: plot-unit length ÷ target chapter length → number of chapters. Use this book’s existing detailed-outline convention for target chapter length; if none exists, use the genre-pace default—about 2,000 Chinese characters for brisk or short-form pacing, and about 3,000 for balanced pacing. If a plot unit exceeds 15,000 Chinese characters and its beats include at least two independent climaxes, split it into multiple plot units; otherwise keep it as one. Split any plot unit longer than 10 chapters into two outlining batches. Both batches share the same retrieval: cap the first batch at 10 chapters, and it may cross into the next plot unit. Retrieve once when creating the card for a plot unit that crosses over; later rolling batches share the plot-unit card’s conclusions for the remaining chapters. The stopping rule remains unchanged; see [Phase 3: Outline Construction](workflow-setup.md#phase-3大纲搭建).

When creating the card, perform one **plot-batch retrieval**: Read “structural distribution” and “plot-point index” from no more than three plot-unit files referenced by the plot-unit card’s “comparable plot reference.” Check the corresponding chapter range in that book’s `剧情/节奏.md` for payoff loops and emotional impact points, plus the emotional-module cards it cites. Extract a common sequence of 5–8 beats and write it into the plot-unit card under “unit beats / chapter-function allocation.” Calibrate the unit emotional-engine field against the reproducible modules in the primary comparable’s `剧情/情绪模块.md`. If the primary artifact exists but one cited emotional-module card is not found, skip only that card. If either `剧情/情绪模块.md` or `剧情/节奏.md` is missing, still follow the fail-fast rule above and repair it first. Follow the same material-replacement and flaw-correction discipline as Steps 3 and 4 above: preserve the emotional chain and functional positions, replace every object, setting, and event, and correct weak handling in place. Once the conclusions are stored in the plot-unit card, individual chapter outlines consume only that card and do not reread the plot unit.

If daily serialization reveals that the volume outline lacks a field required by the current protocol, such as “comparable plot reference,” complete the volume outline before creating the detailed outline. Never write back automatically to a locked volume outline; stop at the outline-completion boundary. Do not write the beat-analysis process into tracking files. Only constraints that ultimately affect future continuity or promises for the next chapter enter the current chapter’s tracking transaction.

## Chapter Function and Rhythmic Variation (Preventing “Every Chapter Feels Like a Short Story”)

The pressure across a volume’s chapters should naturally rise and fall. Here, pressure level means **eruption / conflict intensity**, reusing the suspense scale of 1 minimal / 2 small / 3 moderate / 4 major / 5 extreme—not emotional intensity. A chapter that resolves a relationship or moves readers to tears may have low pressure because it contains little eruption while reaching maximum emotional intensity. The volume outline’s emotional arc records emotional intensity through “emotional tone + intensity.” A chapter’s function emerges naturally from its position inside its level-one structure; it is not a separate label applied afterward. The first chapter of a level-one structure usually establishes setup or empathy, the middle carries the inversion, and the end delivers the eruption and resolution. Chapters such as golden openings or climax settlement + warmth may use compound labels, such as “high pressure + relationship,” or remain blank.

| Chapter Function | Purpose | Pressure Level | Share of Volume (Balanced / Long-Form Baseline, Not a Hard Quota) | Hook Requirement |
|------|------|--------|--------------------------|----------|
| High-pressure chapter | Humiliation / identity reveal / reversal / climax; landing point for a key plot event | 4–5 | About 15–20% | Strong end hook; carries an inversion or turn |
| Standard advancement chapter | Advance the main plot, escalate conflict, or develop the middle of an episode | 2–3 | About 40–50% | Medium end hook; at least one small payoff or increase in anticipation |
| Training-and-testing chapter | Accumulate strength or resources, set up an ability, or fail and experiment | 2 | About 5–10% | Progress hook—almost there / breakthrough imminent; no reversal required |
| Relationship-resolution chapter | Deliver on the romance arc, turn a relationship, or show daily life among close characters | 2–3 | About 5–10% | Emotional hook; push and pull still relates to the main plot |
| Low-pressure life chapter | Breathe after a climax, deepen relationships, and vary the rhythm | 1–2 | No more than about 10% | A weak or purely emotional hook is allowed; no reversal or payoff required |
| Information-organizing chapter | Transition settings, explain the premise, or bridge time | 1 | No more than about 5%; merge when possible and rarely devote a full chapter | No explicit payoff required; convey information through “performance”; end with the next segment’s goal |

Two minimum standards:

- **Continuous anticipation, distributed eruptions.** A low-pressure chapter may lack a payoff or reversal, but every chapter must give readers a reason to continue, such as a small curiosity, stage goal, or romantic anticipation. Eruption intensity falls; forward pull does not. This is especially important for returning readership on Fanqie.
- **Do not cluster emotional themes.** A subtler source of fatigue than “every chapter feels like a short story” is running the same emotional theme at high intensity for many consecutive chapters, such as five straight chapters of patriotic feeling or tearjerking emotion. Separating chapter functions cannot fix that. When arranging the volume outline’s emotional arc, inspect the tone of each group of three or four adjacent chapters. Do not run the same theme for more than two or three chapters consecutively. Differentiate by changing the emotion, hook mechanism, or story line—not by cutting hooks.

Genre tiers (the shares above are a balanced / long-form baseline; adjust by genre): In brisk Fanqie fiction or male-audience career arcs, more than 30% high-pressure chapters and only around 5% breathing chapters can be normal. Do not use the baseline to “add breathing chapters and cut high pressure,” which would reduce retention. Female-audience, social-realism, and regretful-partner fiction can support more relationship and low-pressure chapters because characterization and relationships carry more material, but the story should still follow an emotional zigzag rather than forcing three turns into every chapter. Low-pressure and relationship chapters are part of the appeal—the daily lives of people close to the protagonist can themselves drive subscriptions—not filler.

Connection to length targets: Chapter function determines beat purpose and what to expand or compress, but does not convert each plot point into a fixed word count or add plot points merely to hit a target. After drafting, a program validates against a two-layer length range. If the chapter is clearly short, first determine whether an approved obligation remains unfulfilled. If capacity is insufficient or unclear, negotiate a natural length, outline addition, or new target; never add material automatically. If it is clearly long, perform at most one net-cut compression pass that adds no new meaning and preserves every approved plot point and end hook. If rechecking still falls outside the range, stop instead of entering an endless revision loop.

## Quality-Control Checklist

After completing the outline or structural design, check each item:

- [ ] **Structural level fit**: Does the selected level (one / two / three) fit the genre and length?
- [ ] **Complete causal chain**: Does each act in the five-act structure perform its fixed function, with no skipped steps or altered order?
- [ ] **Every turn has a cause**: Was the cause of every turn planted in advance rather than introduced abruptly?
- [ ] **Increasing difficulty**: Do obstacles rise from low to high without decreasing or remaining constant?
- [ ] **Unbroken hooks**: Does a new major hook begin immediately when the previous one ends, with no dead zone?
- [ ] **No wasted subplots**: Does every subplot connect to the main plot, and do subplot characters serve it?
- [ ] **Reasonable five-level expansion**: Does every lower level serve the level above, without exceeding Level 3 for long-form fiction?
- [ ] **Foreshadowed hidden tracks**: Are hidden tracks foreshadowed from the opening instead of forced in late?
- [ ] **Prepared map changes**: Do relationships move first instead of the story suddenly jumping maps?
- [ ] **Consistent ruler**: Are top-level power benchmarks defined, with boss difficulty in a reasonable range?
- [ ] **Balanced opening and closing of loops**: Is there a plan to close every open loop, with a new one ready when another closes?
- [ ] **Showing-off tracks aligned**: Does showing off trigger when visible combat power > hidden social perception, then raise the hidden track to match?
- [ ] **Distribution of chapter functions**: Does the volume contain layers of high-pressure, advancement, and breathing chapters rather than one constant intensity? Are low-pressure + transition chapters restrained, totaling no more than about 15%?
- [ ] **Continuous anticipation**: Do low-pressure and transition chapters still offer a reason to continue rather than running without hooks? Do adjacent chapters avoid converging emotionally?
- [ ] **Comparable coordinates** (when using a comparable title): Does the quarter point, midpoint, and three-quarter point each anchor one key plot event, with material replaced rather than a specific sequence copied?
