# Reader Contract and Endgame Reserves

This file is the authoritative reference for `story-long-write` on reader contracts, protagonist agency (narrative initiative/control), protection of interests, and stage progression. Its insights were distilled from authorized community samples. Most samples are problem reports or pitfall warnings, so they represent nonstatistical consensus and cannot replace judgments based on the specific genre, platform, and comparable titles.

**Safety boundary**: This file must not contain private source-site data, access credentials, cookies, tokens, request signatures, private identifiers, or any information that could reveal a private account or request environment.

## 1. Core Concepts

### Reader Contract

- Reader contract = the reading satisfaction jointly promised by the opening, genre, protagonist configuration, and narrative voice.
- Before writing, determine what readers came to see: the protagonist solving problems, an ensemble team, a mentor elevating successors, or institutional collaboration and construction. The same event carries different risks under different contracts.
- Genre boundaries precede individual taboos: Introducing a high-level institution—government, sect headquarters, international organization, or multinational corporation—may activate an institutional or faction-scope plotline. First determine whether the opening promised that mode.

### Protagonist Agency: Causal Authority + Settlement Authority

- Evaluate protagonist agency first through **causal authority + settlement authority**, not page time. The protagonist need not personally perform every action or monopolize every scene.
- Irreplaceability begins with causal authority: Through decisions, plans, authorization, irreplaceable information or resources, or active choice of crucial consequences, the protagonist determines why events happen, how they begin, and where they turn.
- Settlement authority = central gains, recognition, power, or crucial consequences return to the rightful recipient under the established promise. The protagonist may leverage others, join a team, be rescued, accept guidance, or delegate local actions, provided causality and settlement are not silently taken away.
- Career and power-fantasy genres are especially sensitive: Readers generally expect the protagonist to control resources, make decisions, bear or choose consequences, and receive visible gains.
- Genre exception—direct wish fulfillment: In Fanqie war-god, live-in-son-in-law, humiliation, and urban-superpower fiction, personally acting and overpowering opponents face-to-face is itself fulfillment of the contract. Agency cannot excuse an idle protagonist while subordinates receive the glory. Only strategy, political intrigue, management, ensemble, and mentor genres assume that the protagonist may operate behind the scenes or deliver through intermediaries.

### Ownership of Anticipation

- A promised fulfillment or spotlight belongs to the protagonist or another promised owner designated for the volume or unit. Supporting characters may perform local actions but cannot silently seize ownership of anticipation.
- Ensemble, mentor, or institutional-collaboration stories may assign ownership explicitly to team members, students, or institutional figures. The key is promising it in advance and settling accordingly, not explaining afterward why stolen attention was justified.

### Four Questions at a Critical Node

Use these questions consistently during outlining, drafting, and review. Answers may point to the protagonist or another owner already promised for the volume; the protagonist need not personally complete every action:

1. Who decides why the event happens and how it begins?
2. Who makes the irreplaceable key choice?
3. Who bears or chooses the crucial consequence?
4. Who ultimately receives the central gain, recognition, or power?

### Protection of Interests and Ownership of Fulfillment

- Protection of interests = readers trust that the central selling point they follow will not be confiscated, diluted, transferred, or handed to an irrelevant character to consume.
- Ownership of fulfillment = the person who received the setup, bore the pressure, and made the choice should receive the main emotional reward. Observer or supporting-character reactions can amplify the protagonist’s payoff but cannot replace it.
- Core assets—special advantage, key resources, identity trump cards, exclusive technology, crucial evidence—must not be handed over without exchange, exposed to an external institution, or taken over by a superior. If surrender is necessary, make the exchange visible: power, protection, resources, channels, recognized status, a claim, information, a stage victory, or initiative in a larger situation.

### Anticipation Debt

- Every planted promise—that the protagonist will counterattack, resources will advance, an identity will be revealed, or a relationship will progress—creates anticipation debt.
- Payment may be delayed, but interest is due: stage-level progress, new information, changed circumstances, emotional release, or a clearer next goal.
- Delaying fulfillment through explanation, worldbuilding, or institutional process increases rationalization debt.

### Endgame Reserves and Progression Pace

The root of “nothing left to write” is exhausting two kinds of **macro resources** too early. It has little to do with how much one chapter advances. Control these two while freeing chapter-level density so the story neither burns out nor frustrates readers.

**Two exhaustible macro resources—define at launch and unlock by volume**

- **Endgame trump cards**: Primary nemesis, ultimate truth/origin, ceiling of the special advantage, final identity/status, and final confirmation of the central relationship. These are single-use resources. At launch, list them under “Endgame Trump Cards and Upgrade Steps” in `设定/题材定位.md` and mark the earliest volume/stage where each may unlock. Work backward from the endgame into volume milestones and unlock only one major milestone per volume.
- **Progression steps**: Remaining tiers of realm, level, map, or faction hierarchy. At launch, perform one staircase check: number of system tiers × length spent at each tier ≥ target book length. Too few steps is a design flaw; fix it during design by extending the system or adding map layers, not by suppressing every chapter. Organize enemies, goals, and maps into **echelons** that unlock gradually. Never leap ahead to instantly defeat the top boss or resolve the ultimate objective.

**Free density within a chapter or plot unit—the source of satisfaction; do not suppress it**

- Give each plot unit one **primary advancement line**—combat power, resources, identity, relationships, information, maps, institutions, factions, career, emotional certainty, or another progression line—which bears the round’s greatest climax. Let other lines receive **spoils** naturally, from a light touch to a major leap. **Multiple gains from one victory—a breakthrough, loot, a faction, reputation, and romance at once—are allowed and often represent strong design. Deliver them when earned.**
- Tier chapter progression by platform and genre: Fast-paced Fanqie and wish fulfillment retain the minimum of “a visible event or payoff advances in every chapter.” Only slow-burn, farming, and emotional fiction relax to “change at least one of goal / risk / information / relationship / resource / identity / emotional alignment.” Use small but visible changes in low-pressure and transition chapters; do not demand a climax every time.
- After a climax or fulfillment, a short low-pressure interval may let readers receive small visible gains or rewards before the next pressure begins. Do not force an immediate new crisis.
- Optional tool: action cost / success cost. An important victory may cost time, relationships, exposure, resources, or new hostility. Do not force a cost into low-pressure, transition, or pure-reward chapters.

**Two overspending questions for detecting excessive progression**

1. Does it use an endgame trump card that should not unlock at this stage—resolve the nemesis, reveal the ultimate truth, reach final status, or cap the special advantage?
2. Does it push a progression line near its ceiling with no remaining step?

Any “yes” = genuine overspending warning → roll back or revise the outline. If both are “no,” allow multiple lines to rise together. This is a heuristic, not a hard threshold; adjust tiers and milestones to the book’s genre, platform, and comparables.

### Rationalization Debt

- Explanation cost establishes why institutional response or control, secrecy, resource movement, cross-jurisdiction enforcement, or organizational collaboration is plausible.
- Check whether the explanation serves the central emotion. If it consumes the payoff, identification, or protagonist spotlight, shorten it first, turn it into a visible exchange, or reduce the institution’s response to observation, obstruction, or a resource interface.

## 2. Institutions and Observers

- Mentioning government, sect headquarters, international organizations, or multinational corporations is not inherently harmful; each is simply a switch for higher-level narration.
- National or government collaboration can be contract-safe if the opening promised institutional cooperation, the protagonist remains irreplaceable, and they gain visible power, protection, resources, returns, or greater freedom of action.
- If the book promises personal ascent, career control, or exclusive ownership of the special advantage, external institutional control of the central problem usually requires a strong exchange and the protagonist’s active choice.
- Appropriate observer/reaction functions: astonishment, misjudgment, spreading public opinion, resource bids, choosing sides, and witnessing the protagonist’s choice. Do not let observers take revenge for the protagonist, make the final judgment, or receive the central gains.

## 3. Risk Levels

| Level | Definition | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Contract-safe | Consistent with the opening genre contract; protagonist retains the key choice or irreplaceable contribution; ownership of gains is clear | Allowed; make the exchange and gains visible |
| Needs reinforcement | May dilute the protagonist’s spotlight or advance too quickly but can be repaired through setup, exchange, cost, or genre declaration | Add rationale or cost before drafting, or make fulfillment stage-level rather than final |
| Contract violation | Confiscates the core selling point; voluntary surrender causes **negative long-term net benefit + loss of protagonist control/causal authority**, especially without visible strategic exchange; an irrelevant character takes ownership of anticipation; a core asset is surrendered or seized without exchange | Revise the outline. A strategic concession may trade local attention or resources for greater control, future gains, relational/institutional power, information advantage, or fulfillment of a promised ensemble/mentor spotlight; surrender alone is not an automatic red light |

## 4. Good and Bad Examples and Exceptions

### Career/Power-Fantasy Protagonist Voluntarily Yields the Spotlight

- Red-light combination: The concession creates negative long-term net benefit, reduces protagonist control/causal authority, and shows no strategic exchange. Not every voluntary concession is a contract violation.
- Good: The protagonist authorizes a subordinate to execute, but controls the plan, crucial resources, final negotiation, and risk; or exchanges local attention/resources for greater control, future gains, relational/institutional power, or information advantage. The subordinate’s performance proves the protagonist’s judgment and organizational ability.
- Exception: If the opening promises “mentorship / organizational management / ensemble growth,” the protagonist’s spotlight may be the training system, eye for talent, and critical rescue.

### Giving Away Resources or Core Assets

- Bad: The protagonist gives exclusive technology, products of the special advantage, or crucial evidence to an institution without conditions, receives verbal thanks, and lets the institution advance the main plot.
- Good: The protagonist receives a license, protection, channels, influence, exclusive returns, or a larger stage while retaining an irreplaceable point of control.

### An Irrelevant Character Takes Revenge for the Protagonist

- Bad: An enemy who oppressed the protagonist for many chapters is defeated by a passing expert or random accident; the protagonist makes no choice, takes no action, and gains no change in perception, resources, or status.
- Good: A supporting character may assist or witness, but the protagonist provides the crucial evidence, plan, decision, public judgment, or final counterattack.
- Exception: Tragedy, mystery, and ensemble war stories may assign local actions to others, but give the protagonist a new core task, cost, or perceptual advance.

### Exhausting Endgame Reserves Early (Multiple Lines Rising Together Is Not a Problem)

- Bad: One plot unit burns every book-level trump card at once—resolves the primary nemesis, reveals the ultimate origin, confirms final identity, and caps the special advantage—leaving no major goal.
- Good: One victory produces multiple gains—breakthrough + loot + reputation—but all remain within the system’s steps and avoid endgame trump cards. The nemesis retreats only one echelon, the truth reveals one layer, and the major reserves remain to repay anticipation debt in later volumes.

### Legitimate Ensemble / Mentor Exceptions

- Ensemble contract: Every POV has its own goal and settlement; the protagonist is not the only payoff source. The volume contract should state who owns the spotlight.
- Mentor contract: The mentor protagonist shines by designing training, recognizing the dilemma, providing critical rescue, and bearing higher-level costs, not by personally claiming every harvest.
- Institutional-collaboration contract: The opening promises government/institutional cooperation. As an expert, commander, core-resource owner, or irreplaceable executor, the protagonist receives explicit power and gains.

## 5. Continuation and Transition Debt

- **Transition debt**: Introducing a new map, institution, ability, enemy, or mystery requires necessary setup and creates more anticipation debt. Fresh elements cannot be used to evade old promises.
- **Genre-specific high risk**: In contract-fulfillment wish fulfillment and power fantasy, a protagonist who repeatedly creates disasters through stupidity or avoidable incompetence and leaves others to clean them up continually damages causal authority, control, and settlement trust. This is high risk under the genre contract, not an absolute prohibition across all stories.
