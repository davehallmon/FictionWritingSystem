## Four Essentials of Writing

### Essential 1: The “And Then?” Baseline
Every paragraph must answer the reader's internal question, “And then?” After delivering one piece of information, immediately connect it to the next.

### Essential 2: Indirect Description
Direct description is only setup; reactions from the side create the satisfying payoff. Describing how a supporting character devours a flatbread or how people scramble for it is a hundred times more effective than dryly saying, “This is the best flatbread in the universe.”

### Essential 3: Design Every Plot Beat Around the Central Emotion
Before writing, identify the target reader's central emotional need (recognition/revenge/romance/advancement). Make every plot beat, character design, and conflict play on that string.

### Essential 4: Control Macro and Micro Together
At the macro level, control overall pacing and emotional direction. At the micro level, control the detail and tension of every paragraph.

## Foundational Writing Techniques

### Plain Description
Plain description = the fewest words + the most precise information and emotion. Use exact verbs and nouns to sketch the image directly, leaving readers room to imagine.

### Five-Senses Method
- Engage at least two or three senses (sight/hearing/touch/smell/taste)
- Write “what is sensed,” not only “what is seen”
- Every sensory detail must serve the emotion—fear uses cold, darkness, and silence; excitement uses brightness, speed, and sound

### Dynamic Description Is Better Than Static Description
- Show character traits through actions and reactions rather than piles of adjectives
- Example: When everyone saw her, their voices unconsciously softened
- Do not elaborate on the environment in long blocks; weave selective details into character action

### Dialogue Guidelines
- Every line must do at least one thing: advance plot / reveal character / create conflict
- Use tone, action, and omission to imply subtext
- Use everyday language rather than a formal written register

### Scene Changes and Transitions
- Transition through a similar object/sensory detail/emotion
- Time shift: connect through an action or object
- Spatial shift: connect through sound or light and shadow

### Camera and Storyboard Thinking
- Every paragraph = one shot with a clear “subject”
- Shot types: wide shot (environment/atmosphere) -> medium shot (relationships) -> close-up (expression/detail) -> extreme close-up (key object/emotion)
- Fast pacing: short sentences, short paragraphs, dense action, rapid cuts
- Slow pacing: long sentences, environmental description, interior thought, static shots

### Building Identification
- The protagonist's objective is clear and earns the reader's support
- Readers understand the motives behind the protagonist's behavior
- The narrative stance must remain on the protagonist's side

### Deepen Characterization vs. Deepen Story
- Deepening story: Complex conspiracies and layered reasoning—a high ceiling but difficult execution
- Deepening characterization: Find drama within relationships and interactions—easier to execute
- The key is making readers feel they have “come to watch the excitement”—character chemistry alone can be compelling

### Writing Socially Intelligent/Brilliant Characters
- Demonstrate intelligence through behavior rather than a narrator's claim
- Extract the core logic of real cases and adapt it to web-fiction context (retain the logical skeleton; replace concrete details)

## Two-Word Prose Acceptance Test

### Word One: Smooth

| Type of Friction | Solution |
|----------|------|
| Overuse of the Chinese aspect particle “了” | Control unnecessary use of “了” |
| Meme-related friction | Use only memes familiar to the target audience |
| Missing-information friction | Supply necessary information at the right time |
| Distant-foreshadowing friction | Briefly remind readers of earlier material before the reveal |

### Word Two: Precise
Give every emotion its proper flavor—let passion feel passionate, warmth feel warm, and horror feel horrifying.

| Emotion | Key | Negative Signal |
|------|------|----------|
| Tragic heroism | Sacrifice must carry weight and its cost must be real | A character dies and readers feel nothing |
| Warmth | Care expressed through everyday details | Forced sentimentality |
| Horror | The unknown and the loss of control | Relying on accumulated gore |
| Passion | A sense of declaration | Empty slogans without setup |

## Camera-Style Writing

Forget omniscience. You are a camera following behind the protagonist. Readers can see only what the protagonist sees.

### Core Principles
1. Write only what can be seen/heard/smelled/touched; do not write “he thought” or “he felt”
2. Every line of dialogue must advance plot, develop character, or convey information
3. Replace description with action: “He was nervous” -> “His hands shook”
4. Prioritize phone-screen readability: Break paragraphs when the action/information changes or when reading catches; split paragraphs, but keep narrative sentences primarily long and comma-linked (see Rule 3 in anti-ai-writing.md for sentence-length standards)

### High-Risk List

**High-risk points of view**
- Omniscient narration: “At this moment, he did not yet know that three days later…”
- Godlike perspective: “If she knew the truth…”
- Psychological analysis: “Deep inside, he actually…”

**High-risk words and phrases**
as though, just like, a trace of, a touch of, took a deep breath, could not help but, a look flashed through their eyes, the corner of their mouth curled, their brow furrowed slightly, brooking no doubt, imperceptible (“seemed” should be judged in context rather than mechanically replaced; slowly, slightly, gently, and faintly are density-controlled, with a combined limit of ≤3 per thousand Chinese characters; isolated natural uses may remain)

**High-risk sentence patterns**
- “…, carrying…” (for example: He said, carrying a trace of helplessness)
- Excessive/formulaic dialogue-tag density -> replace repeated tags with actions and expressions; ordinary “said” may remain
- “He/she knew…” -> show it directly rather than telling
- Clichés/universal metaphors -> replace with an everyday metaphor or plain description

### Replacement Techniques

| Prohibited | Replacement |
|------|------|
| He felt nervous | His hands shook |
| She was very sad | She turned away, her shoulders trembling slightly |
| Repeated formulaic dialogue tags | Connect through action, tone, or context; ordinary “said” may remain |
| He took a deep breath | He crushed the cigarette into the ashtray |
| A trace of surprise flashed through her eyes | Her hand stopped in midair |

### Dialogue Standards
Every line must do at least one thing: advance plot / develop character / create tension

**Dialogue format**
- Break up long speeches with action
- Limit each turn of speech to no more than three sentences
- Avoid frequent/formulaic dialogue tags; ordinary “said” may remain, and should be replaced by action/context only when repetition becomes mechanical or tone monotonous

**Five Escalating Levels of Dialogue Emotion**
1. Objective statement of fact
2. Objective statement + suggestion
3. Subjective accusation
4. Subjective accusation + compulsory command
5. Subjective accusation + manipulative self-elevation (strongest)

### Three-Camera Method

| Camera | Perspective | Content | Function |
|------|------|------|------|
| Camera 1 | Close-up | Protagonist's actions, expressions, thoughts | Advance the main plot and build reader identification |
| Camera 2 | Wide shot | Supporting-character reactions, environmental changes, onlookers | Broaden information and create contrast |
| Camera 3 | Narration | Additional worldbuilding and background | Supply necessary information at the right time |

Alternation rule: Cameras 1 and 2 are primary; weave in Camera 3. Introduce all worldbuilding through conflict.

## Elements of Text

### Core Categories
- **Visual imagery**: Person + environment (static/dynamic), faithfully recorded like a high-definition camera
- **Thought**: Interior activity such as plans/objectives/commentary/conjecture/analysis
- **Emotion**: Hidden within visual imagery and thought rather than stated directly
- **Narration**: Personal history/worldbuilding/relationships/memory, kept as concise as possible

### Levels of Emotional Expression (Low to High)
1. Lowest: Directly write “XX is angry/sad/happy”—tell the emotion, and readers do not feel it
2. Low: Use thought: “XX curses inwardly”
3. Medium: Use expression: “XX's face turns livid”
4. High: Use action: “XX abruptly clenches a fist, nails digging into flesh”

Core principle: The farther an expression stands from the underlying emotion, the more sophisticated it is = indirect description.

### Environmental Interaction (The Core of Environmental Description)
Environmental description without interaction is not worth writing.
- Physical interaction: Pear blossoms drift onto a nose and trigger a sneeze / shivering on a snowy mountain / washing one's face in river water
- Psychological interaction: Food symbolizes a relationship / feeding someone = intimacy / slicing fish with a table knife = threat
- Genre interaction: Cultivation fiction depicts a sky-obscuring kunpeng / wuxia depicts an ordinary man's fury spilling blood within five paces

## Three Elements of Prose and Filler Control

### Three Elements of Prose
- **Precision**: Never use a sentence when one word will do; verb choice determines visual clarity
- **Rhythm**: Default to long, comma-linked narrative sentences; use short sentences only as occasional isolated heavy beats, then return to comma-linked sentences. Avoid uniform length throughout and mechanical alternation between long and short (see Rule 3 in anti-ai-writing.md for sentence-length standards)
- **Visual imagery**: Engage all five senses, not only “seeing”

### Filler Control
- Filler = content that neither advances plot nor develops character
- Justified “filler”: foreshadowing setup/atmosphere/character interaction—an undercurrent is moving
- Harmful filler: repeating known information/meaningless environmental description/a character talking to themselves
- Test: Would deleting this passage confuse readers? -> If not = filler

### Prose Tradeoffs in Long-Form Web Fiction
Long-form fiction does not require exquisite prose; it requires readability and flow. The goal of prose is to make readers forget they are looking at words and see the image directly.

## Comprehensive Prose Analysis and Environmental Interaction

### How the Importance of Prose Changes by Stage

| Stage | Importance | Description |
|------|--------|------|
| Elementary-school prose | 10 points | Without basic competence, nothing else matters |
| Fundamentals achieved | 5 points | Once fundamentals pass, structure and subject selection become the weaknesses |
| Premium level | 7 points | Prose begins to distinguish premium works from one another |
| Ten-thousand-subscriber level | 10 points | Prose is a central competitive advantage for top-tier works |

Assessment: Strong performance but weak prose -> improving prose offers exceptional value; poor performance but strong prose -> improve subject selection and plot.

### Making One Stroke Serve Several Purposes
Let one passage perform multiple functions at once:
- Environmental interaction + characterization + romance thread = three functions in one stroke
- Integrate worldbuilding into plot: Weave the world through small stories
- Plant foreshadowing: Hide information beneath the surface message
- Tight pacing: Hide dry background material inside an interesting minor plot event

### Structural Simplification
The core of an “X-level structure” is “elaboration”: one-sentence main plot -> elaborate -> volume outline -> elaborate -> chapter outline -> elaborate -> manuscript. Working backward is more efficient than working forward.

## The Essence and Levels of Prose

### Three Output Checks
- **Subject selection**: Are the target reader, genre boundaries, and central selling point clear?
- **Plot design**: Can the master/volume/chapter outlines continually generate the target emotion?
- **Prose**: Does the manuscript deliver emotion through concrete actions, dialogue, objects, and rhythm?

If a manuscript problem originates in subject selection or plot design, do not merely polish sentences; return to the corresponding artifact and correct it.

### Levels of Emotional Expression (Low to High)

| Level | Method |
|------|------|
| Lowest | Directly write “XX is angry” |
| Low | Use thought: “XX curses inwardly” |
| Medium | Use expression: “their face turns livid” |
| High | Use action: “abruptly clenches a fist, nails digging into flesh” |

Core principle: Do not write the emotion itself; write its outward effects.

## Point of View and Narration

### Common Points of View in Web Fiction

| Point of View | Characteristics | Best Use |
|------|------|------|
| Third-person limited | Follows the protagonist's perspective | Most common; strongest identification |
| First person | Narrated as “I” | Suspense, slice-of-life fiction |
| Third-person objective | Shows only external behavior | Ensemble casts |
| Shifting viewpoint | Moves among different characters | Create information gaps |
| Omniscient viewpoint | All-knowing | Traditional literature; rarely used in web fiction |

### Usage Guidelines
- Third-person limited is the strongest default for web fiction
- Use viewpoint shifts primarily to create an information gap where “readers know but the protagonist does not”
- Mark viewpoint changes with clear separators
- Use first person cautiously—it limits information and becomes difficult to expand later

### Balancing Abstraction and Specificity
- Formulas are shared conventions; each book's individuality is why readers stay
- Strong web-fiction prose = plain language anyone can understand, cut until only actions and dialogue that drive emotion remain
- Strong web-fiction prose looks plain at first glance and skillful on closer examination

## Analyzing and Avoiding Harmful Reader Triggers

### Five Categories of Harmful Trigger

| Category | Avoidance Method |
|------|----------|
| Protagonist portrayal | Keep the narrative stance on the protagonist's side; the protagonist's actions must receive positive feedback |
| Genre land mines | Understand the target readership and avoid sensitive topics |
| Atmospheric identification | Atmosphere matters a hundred times more than a meme (except in gag-driven fiction) |
| Broken expectations | Control pacing; fulfill an expectation or introduce a new one at the right time |
| Subject selection | Study works with average subscriptions of 3,000–5,000 rather than only market leaders |

### Core Principles
- Cherish the protagonist: The narrative stance must remain on their side
- Define the reading appeal: Harmful triggers vary by genre; satisfy the target audience rather than every reader
- Manage expectations: Repeatedly violating reader expectations creates a crisis of trust
- Keep the opening concise: Show the key elements in Chapter 1

## The Golden Three Chapters = Tone and Expectation Management

The golden three chapters verify that “the actual product matches the picture” by responding quickly to reader expectations.

### Formula
An unsettling situation + status in urgent need of improvement + a hopeful external advantage + a desirable future readers can envision

### Three Levels of Tone
1. Emotional atmosphere
2. Story direction
3. Value orientation

### Two Approaches
- Identification route: Immerse readers in the protagonist quickly
- Anticipation route: Create a crisis at the opening

Rule: Forcing the golden-three-chapter formula can badly damage the tone; do not impose it.

### The Key to Retaining Readers: Unbroken Anticipation
- The protagonist is about to receive something but has not yet obtained it -> anticipation peaks -> continued reading peaks
- Continued reading drops immediately after acquisition -> solution: attach another hook before the acquisition -> repeat
- Keep at least two expectation threads active at all times

## Quality Checklist

After every paragraph/chapter, check each item:

- [ ] **Paragraph control**: Break paragraphs at changes in action/information so reading never catches
- [ ] **High-risk word scan**: Minimize formulaic expressions such as “as though/just like/a trace of/could not help but/a look flashed through their eyes/the corner of their mouth curled”
- [ ] **High-risk pattern scan**: Do not overuse formulaic dialogue tags/“he felt”/“carrying a trace of XX” and similar patterns
- [ ] **Show emotion**: At key emotional beats, do not directly write “he was angry/sad/nervous”; replace it with action/expression. A low-intensity transitional emotion may be stated briefly without externalizing every instance
- [ ] **Effective dialogue**: Every line advances plot/develops character/creates tension
- [ ] **Engage the senses**: Every scene engages at least two senses
- [ ] **Environmental interaction**: Environmental description interacts with a character and is not an empty establishing shot
- [ ] **Camera clarity**: Every paragraph has a clear subject, alternating wide/medium/close/extreme-close shots
- [ ] **Filler check**: Would readers be confused if any paragraph were deleted? If not, it is unnecessary
- [ ] **Consistent viewpoint**: No omniscient narration/godlike viewpoint/psychological analysis slips in
- [ ] **Rhythmic alternation**: Narrative defaults to long, comma-linked sentences (see Rule 3 in anti-ai-writing.md for sentence-length standards); short sentences are occasional isolated heavy beats followed by a return to comma-linked sentences. Avoid uniform sentence length and mechanical long-short alternation; alternate fast and slow scenes
- [ ] **Identification**: The protagonist's goal is clear, behavior understandable, and narrative stance on their side
