# Long-Form Emotional Design: Unit Emotion Engine + Three-Part Method + Failure Modes

> Load when designing emotional story beats. First use the general long-form unit emotion engine below to plan the causal chain. Use the three-part method only as an optional technique when appropriate.

---

## Long-Form Unit Emotion Engine (General Positive Framework)

For each story unit, first design a **positive engine** that generates emotion and delivers a payoff, then conduct a safety review:

> **Core emotional premise → carrier/emotional gap → obstacle or reason the gap persists → current trigger → protagonist’s irreplaceable ignition/transformation action → shift in meaning or visible payoff → genre/contract payoff**

This causal chain applies to different core emotions, including catharsis, excitement, sweetness, heartbreak, fear, and healing. The carrier may be a character, relationship, goal, rule, or scene. If a stage does not apply, mark it as “none/immediate,” but the causal chain must remain closed.

The engine does not require every genre to include a prolonged obstacle, a final image, or a restrained reaction, nor does it prescribe a single mechanism. Misunderstandings, objects, reversals, and deaths are never mandatory. The “Three-Part Heartbreak Method” below is an **optional technique** suited to certain emotional genres, not a rigid template for the general engine.

### Sequence: Positive Engine, Then Negative Guardrails

1. **Design the positive engine first**: Clarify why readers will invest emotionally, how that investment will change meaning during this unit, and how it will receive a visible payoff.
2. **Then use the project’s reader contract as a negative diagnostic guardrail**: Check causal and resolution authority, ownership of expectations, and endgame reserves. Identify contract or progression failures. “Safe” only means that the story has avoided red lines; it does not mean the story is compelling. Guardrails cannot replace the emotional engine.

### Five Plausibility Questions

1. Why is this the right carrier?
2. Why does this happen now?
3. Why was it not resolved directly, or why has it remained unresolved until now?
4. What gives the protagonist the ability or authority to intervene or transform it?
5. How do real-world or setting constraints limit what can happen?

These five questions catch coincidence-driven plot devices, institutions that suddenly become incompetent for the plot’s convenience, and explanatory patches added after the fact.

### Intensity Principles

Strong emotion comes from a genre-appropriate combination of mechanisms such as **specific investment, obstruction/delay, changes in meaning, and credible reactions**. It does not require every mechanism, nor does it come from piling up death, illness, and crying.

Restrained reactions are only an optional amplifier for certain emotions. Objects, dialogue, actions, identities, rules, and relationships may all serve as optional anchors. Adjust anchor frequency to the story’s length and the reader’s memory needs; never require an anchor to appear exactly three times.

Collective or thematic elevation is also optional. Expand an individual payoff into the genre’s core emotion only when the genre requires it.

## Three-Part Heartbreak Method

### Part One: Bond Building (First Third)

**Goal**: Use concrete objects, numbers, and details to give the relationship texture and convince readers that it is real.

**Methods**:

- Use specific numbers to establish a sense of time: “eight years together,” “five years in a coma,” “seven long years.”
- Use specific objects to carry emotion: a gold lock pendant given by an older sister for a birthday, figures in a ledger recording repaid debt, or a small wooden horse carved smooth by a king.
- Use repeated actions to establish habits: one bowl of congee every day, a visit every two weeks, or being too cold to sleep each winter.

**Examples**:

- *Twenty Years Too Late*: Twenty years of ledger entries totaling eighty thousand yuan establish the weight of a mother-daughter relationship.
- *I Prayed for You Before the Buddha*: Five years in a coma, details of praying to gods and Buddhas, and a secret love that began in middle school.
- *The Imperial Brother Bullied the Young Prince*: Concrete hardships from seven years as a political hostage—kneeling in the snow, being slapped, and eating spoiled food.

**Core principle**: The more concrete the bond, the more painful the later rupture. Do not rely on abstractions such as “they loved each other very much.”

---

### Part Two: Emotional Rupture (Middle and Later Sections)

**Goal**: Create contrast so readers believe they were right to hate someone, then discover they hated the wrong person—or the reverse.

**Methods**:

- **Contrast method**: Show warmth first, then destroy it with a cruel truth.
- **Misalignment method**: Character A believes they are protecting Character B but is actually hurting them.
- **Delayed-truth method**: Reveal critical information at the moment readers least expect it.

**Examples**:

- *My Brother-in-Law Charged Me for Delivering Baby Formula*: The mother appears weak and pitiable but has actually been manipulating the older sister into demanding money. The gold lock pendant is fake—gold-plated copper—and the contrast tears the relationship apart.
- *The Imperial Brother Bullied the Young Prince*: Lady Wang appears gentle, bringing a hand warmer and preparing medicine, but she has actually drugged the late empress into madness and driven her to death with deliberately cruel words.
- *My Reborn Younger Brother*: The younger brother deeply loves his girlfriend, but she is a scammer, the pregnancy is fake, and he is deceived into entering a telecom-fraud compound.

**Core principle**: The strength of the rupture depends on the depth of the setup. Without a well-established bond, the rupture has no roots.

---

### Part Three: Lingering Ache (Ending)

**Goal**: Break through the reader’s defenses with a quiet detail rather than loud grief.

**Methods**:

- Use an ordinary action to carry enormous emotion: continue feeding someone congee, fold the clothes neatly, or leave without looking back.
- Use an object to create a lingering aftereffect: a broken gold lock pendant, a wooden horse, or a bloodstained ring.
- Use “not” to create negative space: do not explain, do not look back, do not cry.

**Examples**:

- *The Imperial Brother Bullied the Young Prince*: “He held the little wooden horse I had polished smooth... It fit perfectly in his hand.”
- *My Brother-in-Law Charged Me for Delivering Baby Formula*: “My phone became much quieter. The troubles that had tormented me for so long suddenly seemed very, very small.”
- *My Prospective Daughter-in-Law Scored Us*: “After the adoption was finalized, she stepped into this unfamiliar home for the first time... I knew I had not chosen wrong this time.”

**Core principle**: The best emotional aftereffects do not depend on loud sobbing. They stay with readers long after the story ends.

## Emotional Push-Pull Rhythm Design

### Basic Rhythm Curve

```
温暖 → 残忍 → 善意 → 真相 → 原谅 → 来不及 → 释然 → 细节暴击
```

<!-- translation-companion: non-executable -->
```text
Warmth → Cruelty → Kindness → Truth → Forgiveness → Too Late → Acceptance → Devastating Detail
```

Not every story should use the entire curve. Choose a subset according to genre:

| Genre | Typical Rhythm | Core Push-Pull |
|------|----------|----------|
| Social drama/cathartic fiction | Oppression → Endurance → Eruption → Humiliation of the antagonist | Fast emotional rebound and frequent comeuppance |
| Emotional/heartbreak fiction | Sweetness → Rupture → Attempted repair → Missed chance | Slow setup with a lasting emotional impact |
| Historical romance/revenge | Endurance → Strategy → Exposure → Retribution | Simple setup and aesthetically forceful violence |
| Mystery/detective fiction | Calm → Unease → Truth → Shock | Information gaps create unease |
| Period/family fiction | Misunderstanding → Conflict → Truth → Reconciliation | Generational conflict and a strong sense of period |

### Push-Pull Principles

1. **The frequency of emotional turns is a genre- and length-based rule of thumb**: Adjust it according to genre, length, and the established payoff rhythm. Do not require a turn every three to five sections.
2. **Every emotional turn must have a triggering event**. Emotion cannot change without a reason.
3. **The final turn determines the emotional tone that lingers after the ending.**
4. **Cathartic fiction permits a rapid rebound; heartbreak fiction requires a longer setup.**

## Genre-Specific Emotional Strategies

### Social Drama/Cathartic Fiction

- Emotional rebound speed: Fast—deliver comeuppance within two or three sections after humiliation.
- Comeuppance density: Once every three to five sections.
- Antagonist’s arrogance: Escalating—minor arrogance at first, extreme arrogance at the end.
- Ending tone: Exhilarating and satisfying.
- Example: *My Prospective Daughter-in-Law Scored Us*—turning the scoring system against her, the zero in the metal box, and severing the relationship.

### Emotional/Heartbreak Fiction

- Bond-detail density: High—a deep bond must be established in the first third.
- Contrast design: Warm before cold, sweet before bitter.
- Lingering-effect technique: Quiet ending plus an object detail.
- Ending tone: Lingering regret and acceptance.
- Example: *My Brother-in-Law Charged Me for Delivering Baby Formula*—the truth about the gold lock pendant → false love → independence.

### Historical Romance/Revenge

- Simple-setting principle: Keep relationships clear; do not create an unnecessarily complex world.
- Aesthetic treatment of violence: Make the retaliation direct and decisive.
- Trump-card timing: Reveal the greatest trump card in the final quarter.
- Ending tone: Deep satisfaction and karmic retribution.
- Example: *The Imperial Brother Bullied the Young Prince*—an order for twenty strikes → investigation reveals the truth → the empress is deposed.

### Mystery/Detective Fiction

- Information-gap design: Readers know something the character does not, or the reverse.
- Process-of-elimination structure: Gradually eliminate possibilities until only the truth remains.
- Motive-reveal rhythm: Reveal what happened first, then why it happened.
- Ending tone: Unsettling implications or sudden clarity.
- Example: *My Reborn Younger Brother*—rebirth premise + scammer identity + telecom-fraud compound.

### Period/Family Fiction

- Generational conflict: Do not take sides; show the suffering on both sides.
- Period texture: Use era-specific objects and customs.
- Reconciliation rhythm: Do not reconcile too quickly. Allow both sides to be fully hurt first.
- Ending tone: Warmth tinged with regret.
- Example: *My Brother-in-Law Charged Me for Delivering Baby Formula*—parent-child relationship → truth about the gold lock pendant → rupture.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure Mode | How to Identify It | Corrective Direction |
|----------|----------|----------|
| **Too flat** | Five or more consecutive sections without an emotional turn | Introduce an unexpected event or new information |
| **Too rushed** | A major turn lacks recognizable setup | Provide enough clear setup for the turn’s weight and the story’s length; do not impose a fixed three-section rule |
| **Hollow suffering** | Readers feel discomfort but no compassion | Check whether the bond was established through concrete details |
| **Disjointed** | The first and second halves feel like different stories | Carry a clue, object, or theme across both halves |
| **Collapsed ending** | The story keeps repeating explanations long after the reversal | Resolve or cool down at a pace appropriate to the genre and length; do not impose a fixed five-hundred-character limit |
| **Broken characterization** | A character’s behavior at a critical moment conflicts with their established characterization | Review the characterization and restore behavioral logic |

## Quick Self-Check

Use this checklist when designing emotion:

- [ ] Does the first third contain specific details that establish the bond rather than abstract claims?
- [ ] Does the middle contain contrast or rupture—readers believe A, but the truth is B?
- [ ] Does the ending contain a quiet detail rather than a long emotional monologue?
- [ ] Is the frequency of emotional turns adjusted to the genre, length, and established payoff rhythm rather than a fixed density?
- [ ] Does the antagonist’s behavior follow their characterization?
- [ ] Is the story free of the six failure modes above?
