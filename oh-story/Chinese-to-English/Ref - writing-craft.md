## 1. Grounding Emotion: Do Not Default to Micro-Movements

### Rule

Emotion has no fixed translation into prose. If context already makes readers feel it, do not add another reaction. If emotion drives plot, prioritize the character's choice, dialogue, strategy, relationship change, object change, or concrete consequence. When a direct sentence is most accurate, state it (“He was tired of listening”). Physical state is only one option; use it only when it adds information, affects action, or genuinely belongs to this character and scene.

### Implementation Order

At an emotional beat, evaluate in this order rather than “replacing emotional words one by one”:

1. **Context already establishes it**: If prior conflict, dialogue, or result is enough for readers to understand, delete the added explanation; do not append another action.
2. **Emotion changes the next step**: Write what the character says or withholds, which route they choose, which strategy they change, or which cost they pay.
3. **An in-scene object can carry the change**: Show what happens to the contract, medicine, meal, door, message, or tool already being handled. The object must come from the scene; do not import a teacup merely to perform emotion.
4. **The body is genuinely affected**: Show a failed action, pain, imbalance, injury, or visible consequence. “A fingertip moves, knuckles whiten, lips press together, eyes shift, breath catches” without consequence can usually be deleted.
5. **Direct statement is more precise and efficient**: A low-intensity or transitional emotion may be stated in one sentence; do not stage a thirty-character performance merely to avoid an ordinary emotion word.

Choose only the most informative option for one beat unless escalating emotion genuinely produces consecutive consequences. Do not stack “action + body + metaphor + psychological explanation” into four layers of annotation.

### Functions of a Strong Detail

| Situation | Handling |
|------|------|
| Context already shows impatience | Enter the next line of dialogue directly, or summarize with “He was tired of listening” |
| Anger changes the negotiation | Show the character withdrawing the offer, halting cooperation, or confronting the other person; the change carries more information than a clenched fist |
| Anxiety disrupts the current task | Show a pen tip ruining a signature or a key entering the wrong lock—specific consequences |
| A bodily detail merely labels emotion at sentence end | Delete it; do not replace it with another body part or synonymous action |

### Four Anti-Cliché Questions

Review important emotional passages chapter by chapter and identify the relevant source sentence; this does not require adding a reaction to every paragraph:

1. If this bodily/vocal detail is deleted, is any information, choice, relationship, or physical outcome lost? If not, delete it.
2. Would it still work after changing the name and room? If yes, it may be generic performance rather than character-specific detail.
3. Does the chapter repeatedly use “body part + slight action/state + once,” especially fingertips, knuckles, gaze, lips, breath, and cuffs? When matched, rewrite or delete the full passage rather than rotating synonyms.
4. Has dialogue, context, and action already explained the same emotion repeatedly? Keep only the strongest instance.

## 2. Recurring Object System (Three-Appearance Rule)

### Rule

Design one or two recurring objects for every narrative unit (short story/long-form volume). Each object must appear three times with a different meaning each time.

### Three-Appearance Sequence

| Appearance | Function | Example |
|----------|------|------|
| First appearance (first quarter) | Establish initial meaning | Gold lock = birthday gift from the older sister (warmth) |
| Second appearance (midpoint turn) | Overturn the meaning | Gold lock = brass plated with gold (falsehood) |
| Third appearance (ending) | Deliver the emotional blow | Gold lock is thrown into the trash (complete rupture) |

### Object-Design Template

Fill in the following template and write it into 设定.md:

```
物件：{名称}
第1次出现：第{N}节 | {场景} | 含义：{初始意义}
第2次出现：第{N}节 | {场景} | 含义：{转折意义}
第3次出现：第{N}节 | {场景} | 含义：{结尾意义}
```

### Quick Reference: Object Types

| Type | Characteristic | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Token | Symbolic object carrying a relationship | Gold lock (delivered by brother-in-law), small wooden horse (imperial younger brother) |
| Tool | Functional object used in daily life | Scorebook (prospective daughter-in-law), account ledger (twenty years late) |
| Trace | Lingering bodily/environmental mark | Old wrist scar, height marks on a wall |
| Number | Concrete amount/duration carrying a relationship | 80,000-yuan bill, twenty years, 800 yuan |

## 4. Numbers/Amounts as Narrative Tools

### Rule

Replace vague descriptions with specific numbers. Numbers carry emotional weight, and changing numbers advance plot.

### Operating Instructions

| Scene | Operation | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Establish weight | Replace “a long time” / “a lot” with a specific amount or duration | “In love for eight years,” not “together for a long time” |
| Escalate harm | Increase the number each time | 800 → 1,000 → 80,000 → 1.2 million |
| Devastating contrast | After extreme inflation, plunge to a tiny value | “My sister borrowed eighty thousand yuan to put me through school. My brother-in-law transferred one yuan to me.” |
| Weight of time | Use duration to carry emotion | “After twenty years, she called me Mom for the first time” |

### Numerical-Narrative Template

```
数字线：
第{N}节 | {数字1} | 含义：{初始}
第{N}节 | {数字2} | 含义：{升级}
第{N}节 | {数字3} | 含义：{暴击/反转}
```

## 5. Vary Action and Stillness Without Section Quotas

### Rule

A chapter as a whole needs rising and falling movement, but every section does not need its own “action” and “stillness.” A pause may come from suspended information, a gap in dialogue, environmental pressure, or temporary inaction; it does not require tiny actions such as wiping dust or straightening a collar.

### Categories of Action and Stillness

| Type | Content | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Action | Physical impact, violence, eruption of conflict | Slashing someone, smashing objects, slapping, coughing blood, flipping a table |
| Stillness | Suspended information, silence in dialogue, environmental pressure, waiting, or a necessary daily process | The reply never arrives; no one at the table answers; rain masks footsteps outside the door |

### Operating Instructions

- Arrange action and stillness according to the full chapter and scene; do not set numerical quotas for each section
- When many consecutive sections are all “action,” check for violence fatigue; intentional continuity in a chase/fight may remain
- When many consecutive sections are all “stillness,” check for stagnation; intentionally extended waiting/oppression may remain
- Emotional intensity does not map to fixed action types: An explosive beat can become suddenly silent, and a low point can contain sustained labor

### Pacing Example

```
节5（动）：萧衍一声令下，打了萧琅二十棍
节6（静）：报数停了，殿里没人替萧琅求情
节7（动）：王氏扑过来，跪在地上磕头
节8（静）：王氏跪在原地，只听见宫门落锁
```

## 6. Event Density in the Opening

### Rule

The first 100 Chinese characters must contain ≥3 events. Do not establish background first; begin with an event chain.

### Operating Instructions

- Delete all opening background introductions (“In the ancient XX era,” “She was an XX kind of girl”)
- Make the first sentence an event, not description
- Event-density check: Count independent “things that happen” in the first 100 Chinese characters

### Event-Density Example

```
❌ 低密度（1 件事/100 字）：
「沈栀是沈家的嫡女，自幼聪慧，深得父亲喜爱。这一天，她收到了一道圣旨。」

✅ 高密度（4 件事/50 字）：
「萧衍回朝那天，母后已经死了，儿子被他皇弟打成痴傻，他提刀就进了宫。」
（回朝 → 母死 → 子被虐 → 打人，4 件事）
```

Density means how many events occur in a passage, not splitting one event into disconnected one-sentence lines. The example joins four events into one sentence with commas and remains high-density.

### Density-Check Template

After writing the opening, list its first 100 Chinese characters' events:

```
事件1：{事件}
事件2：{事件}
事件3：{事件}
（如果列出 < 3 个，重写开头）
```

## 7. Section-Density Diagnosis

### Rule

If a completed section feels short, use this diagnostic checklist. Never add description merely to reach a length.

### Diagnostic Checklist

Is the completed section too short? Check in order:

| Check | Reason It Is Short | Remedy |
|--------|----------|------|
| Is the key subevent understandable? | The immediate scene, causality, or character's next step lacks necessary information | Add only the genuinely missing dimension; do not add sensory or bodily actions merely to complete all three (see Section 8) |
| Does dialogue have only one or two exchanges? | Dialogue lacks confrontation | Add one exchange of power-struggle dialogue (see Section 3) |
| Is emotional information grounded? | It only labels emotion or repeats it with a purposeless micro-movement | Prioritize choice, dialogue, object, and consequence; state it directly when needed, and require bodily detail to serve a function (see Section 1) |
| Still short and the key beat is thin? | Conflict, choice, cost, or relationship change is underdramatized | Return to existing outline events and add confrontation/consequences; if no content exists, expand the outline rather than padding with senses/memory |

### Expanding When There Are Too Few Subevents

When an outline lacks subevents, choose in this order:

```
IF 主事件是冲突/对抗 → 加「阻碍」：主角被中途打断或阻止
IF 主事件涉及配角 → 加「反应」：配角的意外反应
IF 主事件有空间移动 → 加「发现」：移动途中发现新线索
IF 主事件触发回忆 → 加「倒叙」：插入一段 2-3 句的简短回忆
IF 主事件是一连串动作 → 加「递进」：动作引发的连锁反应
```

### Task Obstacles: Use Only When “The Task Would Naturally Be Blocked”

A task obstacle is neither additional process writing nor an attempt to look natural; it blocks a character pursuing a concrete objective and produces a change.

Diagnostic sentence: The character already needs to do this → this step is blocked → the character chooses/pays a cost → the obstacle changes information, relationships, cost, choice, or foreshadowing.

When useful:
- Main event is confrontation: The obstacle exposes the opponent's power, a loophole in rules, or a hidden countermove.
- Main event is verification: The obstacle forces out new evidence, a new witness, or a new information gap.
- Main event advances a relationship: The obstacle forces a character to state a position, help, refuse, or betray.
- Main event is a low-pressure transition: The obstacle supplies only light resistance and serves relationship, foreshadowing, or everyday texture without overwhelming the main event.

Deletion test: If removing it loses no information, emotion, relationship, cost, or foreshadowing, it is not useful. Do not add process merely to look natural or increase length.

### Anti-Padding Rules

The following are prohibited:
- Adding environmental description merely for length (“Bright sunlight shone outside the window”)
- Repeating an emotion already expressed merely for length
- Adding summarizing interior monologue merely for length
- Making a character perform a meaningless action merely for length
- Adding procedural detail to look natural; delete it when nothing is lost
- Reexplaining emotion for length or appending one purposeless bodily reaction to every paragraph

## 8. Writing Scenes (Weaving in Three Dimensions)

### From Detailed Outline to Manuscript: Dramatize the Specification; Do Not Copy Its Shape

The fields in a detailed outline specify **what must happen** in the chapter; they are **not the shape of the manuscript**. Filling prose box by box in the outline's shape produces telegram-like exposition, one flat beat per sentence—the “copied outline shape,” an AI-like pattern just like copying an example's shape.

- A plot-beat sequence is a list of “what must happen,” not a sentence template: Do not write one sentence or paragraph per beat and advance rigidly in order. Dramatize each point as a choice, action, line, object, or result the character can perceive now, using a physical reaction only when needed—**dramatize it; do not report it**.
- If one constraint repeats under “Central Event / Content Summary / Plot Arrangement / Plot Beats,” merge it into **one semantic point** before generation. Repetition among fields is neither emphasis nor permission to restate it four times. The manuscript must not mechanically repeat outline wording.
- The five-part structure (cause/development/turn/climax/ending) is a functional breakdown for the outline author, not five manuscript paragraphs. Prose need not proceed in that order or map one paragraph to each box.
- Summary sentences of “who did what,” arrow chains such as “A→B→C,” and “the state where this resolves” are planning language and must not be copied into narration.
- Plot beats may be combined, interwoven, and reordered so adjacent points form a continuous scene. Expand dense points into slow motion and summarize sparse points (see “Density Distribution”).
- End on the final concrete action, image, line, or suspense beat. “Ending design/resolved state” is planning language; never turn it into a summary such as “and so…” or “he finally understood…” (see anti-ai-writing.md for removing end-of-chapter uplift).

### Viewpoint Stance: Deep Limited Knowledge (Grounded Immersion)

Before weaving three dimensions, establish narrative stance. By default, lock to the viewpoint character's “present perception”—write only what they currently see, hear, smell, feel physically, or briefly think. This simultaneously resolves two persistent AI-like traits: weak immersion and an “author present” voice that lectures or speaks omnisciently.

- **Never pull the camera back, look down from above, or enter another person's mind**: Instead of the camera-like “the entire hall fell into dead silence,” write “she heard her heartbeat; the breathing beside her vanished all at once.”
- **Reader and character learn together**: Do not write what the character does not know, reveal information early, or fill background. Suspense comes from “she does not know either”; prohibit advance-reveal lines such as “what she did not know was.”
- **Thought is part of action**: Present psychology through “flash thought + body”—a half sentence, interrupted, biased by emotion, rather than a complete rational internal monologue.
- **Use subjective bias instead of objective narration**: Color the scene with character emotion. Someone she hates appears loathsome; when she panics, the light wavers. Do not use neutral description that would look the same to everyone. It must remain her instantaneous biased perception, not an objective verdict such as “as though pronouncing judgment on something long decided” (that is the author entering to define it; see Pattern 8 in anti-ai-writing.md).

Deep limited knowledge is the root of “natural AI removal”: Nail the camera inside the character's body and the author has nowhere to step out and explain, summarize, or arrange events (together with Pattern 8 in anti-ai-writing.md).

### Style Fingerprint: Borrow Genre Cadence, Not a Universal Voice

Real cadence differs by genre and changes the reading experience; effective methods differ too. Male-oriented/military fiction may borrow the continuous narrative momentum of older web fiction; modern everyday fiction can follow ordinary tasks. Suspense, science fiction, and historical fiction must not force a Coiling Dragon voice, old-web-fiction voice, or first-person register. First read this book's `设定/文风.md`, comparable deconstruction, and matching chapter summary.

Implementation order: First write smoothly in this chapter's emotion and genre style, then address explanatory summary, overcompression, and piled metaphors. Do not mechanically lengthen/shorten sentences or globally replace diction with a comparable title's lexicon.

### Rule

A key subevent cannot remain a single outline-summary sentence. Occurrence is the trunk; add perception and reaction only when they supply new information, weaving them into one shot rather than separating them by dimension or requiring all three.

### Rules for Weaving Three Dimensions

Occurrence, perception, and reaction are optional information dimensions, not per-paragraph quotas. A fully dramatized key subevent can weave whichever dimensions contain real content into the same shot. Leave a dimension empty when context already makes it clear; never add sensory or bodily action merely to complete all three. Transitional, travel, or informational subevents can pass in one or two sentences (see “Density Distribution”), reserving length for emotional beats.

| Dimension | Content | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| Occurrence | Something happens | One or two sentences containing one concrete detail |
| Perception | What the protagonist notices | Select one sound, object, spatial detail, or bodily sensation that changes understanding, judgment, or atmosphere; never fill a sensory quota |
| Reaction | What the character does next | May be a choice, dialogue, strategy, task action, explicit feeling, or consequential physical response; do not default to micro-movements |

For a task obstacle, use only needed dimensions: occurrence = the task cannot proceed; perception = concrete evidence such as a returned paper, withheld object, or half-finished line; reaction = the character changes strategy, pays a cost, asks for help, forces through, yields, or keeps score. Do not explain “why the task is blocked” like a manual or append an action at scene end to explain meaning.

### Antipattern: Stacked Description (Absolutely Prohibited)

Writing the three dimensions as three sequential paragraphs is one of AI's most common stylistic traces. Readers see one action split and written three times:

> ❌ **Stacked (incorrect)**:
> Father Lin lowered his head, held the document down with his left hand, and lowered the pen to the paper with his right.
>
> His hand was shaking.
>
> His arm shook from elbow to wrist. The pen paused on the page, drew one horizontal stroke, then stopped again. The left-falling stroke in the character “Lin” came out crooked. He paused, pressed the pen down, and rewrote it. When he finished, the pen was still unsteady in his hand.

→ One event becomes three paragraphs—occurrence→perception→reaction—with the same action split three ways

### Correct Approach: Woven Description

Weave needed dimensions into one continuous paragraph so readers experience one complete moment:

> ✅ **Woven (correct)**:
> Father Lin held the document down with his left hand and lowered the pen with his right. The point veered as soon as it touched the page, his arm shaking uncontrollably from elbow to wrist, dragging the horizontal stroke sideways. He paused, pressed down, and rewrote it. The left-falling stroke in the finished character “Lin” was crooked, and the pen still would not steady in his hand.

→ Here the bodily reaction changes the result on the page and carries information; it is not an added micro-movement used merely to prove nervousness

### Self-Check Method

For a fully dramatized key scene, use the “three-dimension covering test”: Cover occurrence, perception, and reaction in turn, then decide whether each existing element contributes different information. A missing dimension does not mean it must be added; weave it into the same shot only when readers would otherwise fail to understand the scene, causality, or the character's next step. If covering one bodily/vocal detail loses nothing, delete it. Also perform the Section 1 “Four Anti-Cliché Questions” chapter by chapter and identify how each source sentence was handled; “complied” is not a substitute for review.

### Visual Paragraphing (Prevent Wall-to-Wall Paragraphs)

Weaving three dimensions does not mean placing one subevent into one long paragraph. Weaving solves “one moment split across occurrence/perception/reaction paragraphs”; paragraph breaks solve “a paragraph too dense for phone reading.”

Break paragraphs at changes in shot/information, not changes in dimension:

| Trigger | Paragraph-Break Method |
|---|---|
| A new action begins | Start a paragraph so readers see the next beat |
| A new object/clue appears | Start a paragraph dedicated to that information |
| The character's gaze moves | Start a paragraph to create a cut |
| Direct quotation begins | Give dialogue its own paragraph |
| A thought/judgment lands | It may stand alone to create a pause |

Decision rules:

- One paragraph carries one shot/one action unit, or one unfinished chain of reasoning, atmosphere, or emotion.
- Ordinary narrative paragraphs may be short, but sentences within them must not fragment—do not give every action its own period (narrative defaults to longer comma-linked sentences; see Rule 3 in anti-ai-writing.md). Detailed description, reasoning, oppressive atmosphere, and emotional settling may run longer as long as they still address one event.
- Length is diagnostic rather than mandatory: Split only when reading becomes crowded or several actions/information shifts/gaze shifts appear; do not force a break before a complete dramatic unit ends merely to shorten it.
- Ordinary prose alternates short paragraphs, slightly longer paragraphs, dialogue, and action paragraphs. High-pressure conflict, comeuppance, and twists may become more fragmented and brief.
- Avoid several consecutive paragraphs of identical length and never divide a complete reasoning chain into mechanical fragments.

Before output, perform one natural-rhythm rearrangement:

1. Scan every natural paragraph; first ask, “Does this paragraph complete only one event/shot/reasoning or emotional chain?” If yes, retain it and do not split based only on length.
2. If the paragraph introduces a new action, object, information, dialogue, gaze shift, or scene ending, break there.
3. If several consecutive very short paragraphs still belong to the same shot/event, merge adjacent sentences so the prose does not resemble an outline or poem.
4. If one long sentence catches when read aloud or overloads information, first remove modifiers or split the sentence; after splitting, keep the dramatic unit intact within one paragraph.

### Subject and Name Rhythm (Prevent “Excessive Subjects”)

The protagonist's/character's name serves “subject reset” and “emphasis,” not a label for every sentence:

- Use the protagonist's name at paragraph openings, scene changes, scenes with several characters, and viewpoint resets to establish the subject.
- Within one action chain/paragraph, favor “he/she,” action continuity, or omitted subjects so sentences flow naturally.
- Name the protagonist again at a key turn, emotional eruption, identity contrast, or when readers must refocus on them.
- Negative signal: Several consecutive sentences or paragraphs begin with the same protagonist name without ambiguity, scene change, or need for emphasis.
- Do not judge mechanically by total name count; the final standard is whether reading catches because “every sentence reports the name.”

Incorrect fix: Splitting a complete reasoning/atmosphere chain into several paragraphs merely to shorten it; omitting subjects until references become unclear in a multicharacter scene.

Correct fix: Preserve continuity within one event, break at a new action/object/information/dialogue, establish the subject by name at paragraph start, flow through pronouns/omission within the paragraph, and name again at the key turn.

### Density Distribution (Uneven Emphasis)

Do not give every beat identical length and detail—uniform effort creates AI-like prose and the feeling that “everything is full but nothing stands out.” Allocate prose according to plot weight:

| Beat Type | Prose Density | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff/comeuppance/twist/emotional climax | Dense (detailed) | Fill with perception, action, and dialogue confrontation; expand beat by beat in slow motion |
| Transition/travel/information/time jump | Sparse (summary) | Pass in one or two sentences, even one summary line; do not expand all three dimensions |
| Setup/everyday life/relationship warming | Medium | Render one or two immersive details and summarize the rest |

Principle: Concentrate detailed writing at emotional beats and compress transitions decisively. Readers remember images in dense passages; sparse passages move them quickly toward the next payoff. Varied density lets pacing breathe.

### Connecting Subevents

Connect subevents through their actual relationship: causal progression, dialogue response, new information, spatial movement, or the character's next step. No rule requires an inserted physical action or a fixed transition length.

- ❌ Added solely as a transition: “I set the ledger on my knees, a thin film of sweat covering my palm.”
- ✅ “The final expense had no receipt, but his signature was tucked into the next page.” (New information directly advances the next beat)

### Perception Reference

| Scene Type | Available Perceptual Detail |
|---|---|
| Reading/reviewing | Darkness of handwriting, paper texture, bleeding ink, curled page corners |
| Dialogue | Changes in expression, pauses in tone, silence in the air |
| Memory | One suddenly clear detail in the image (smell/sound/touch) |
| Interior | Changes in light, object placement, temperature |
| Movement | Footsteps, feel of the ground, wind direction |

### Boundaries of the Three Dimensions

- ✓ Perception is a detail the protagonist actively notices (a focus of attention)
- ✓ Reaction is the character's next step after being affected, expressed as choice, dialogue, strategy, task action, explicit feeling, or consequential physical change
- ✗ Perception cannot be decorative scene description (“Sunlight spilled across the windowsill”)
- ✗ Reaction cannot use a purposeless micro-movement to label an already clear emotion again
- ✗ The three dimensions cannot repeat the same information

## 9. Tone-Based Punctuation Spectrum (Punctuation Follows Voice)

### Rule

Punctuation carries tone, power relationships, and emotional beats; it is not decoration. While drafting, first decide “what this sentence does in the scene,” then choose a period, comma, question mark, exclamation point, colon, or line break/action beat. Do not use ellipses or dashes to manufacture pauses.

### Punctuation Spectrum

| Tone / Function | Available Punctuation | Operating Instruction | Prohibition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure / calm / restraint | Periods, commas, colons, short sentences | Apply pressure through understated statements and land judgments with colons; explain less | Do not add `！` randomly merely “for variety” |
| Challenge / probing / rhetorical question | Question marks, short follow-up lines, action pauses | Use `？` for a key question; a follow-up may stand as a short line | Do not write five consecutive sentences ending in `？` |
| Surprise / eruption / comeuppance | A few exclamation points, standalone sentences | Use one `！` only at an emotional peak; support the explosive beat with short sentences before and after | Prohibit `!!!` and paragraph-long shouting |
| Hesitation / unfinished speech / guilt | Commas, periods, short sentences, actions such as swallowing/stopping a hand | Pause through action or changed sentence length; use another short sentence when needed | Do not use `……` as a substitute for a pause |
| Interruption / drawn-out sound | Interrupting action, line break, short sentence, unfinished action | Cut off speech with an action such as “He raised a hand and stopped her.” | Use no `——`/`—`/`--` in narration or dialogue |
| Information reveal / landing a judgment | Colons, semicolons, short sentences | “There was only one piece of evidence: The door was bolted from inside.” A colon can create the landing | Do not write academic chains of semicolons |

### Execution Steps

1. Read the scene's target emotion and character voice, then label every key dialogue beat as one tone (pressure/probing/eruption/hesitation/interruption).
2. During refinement, scan sentence endings: If an entire passage uses only periods, check whether challenges, hesitation, and explosive beats were flattened. If `？`/`！` fill the screen, remove all but functional instances.
3. Replace every `……`, `——`, `—`, and `--` in prose (including dialogue) with periods, commas, short sentences, line breaks, or action-based interruption. Salted Words `「」` are only a quotation style and do not affect legitimate use of question or exclamation marks.
